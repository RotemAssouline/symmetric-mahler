Statement

A convex body is a compact convex set \(K\subset\mathbb{R}^d\) with nonempty interior; it is symmetric if \(K=-K\). Recall that the polar body \(K^\circ\) of a convex body \(K\) containing the origin in its interior is defined by \[K^{\circ}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^d\colon\langle x,y\rangle\le1\ \ \forall\,x\in K\}.\]

Theorem

For every symmetric convex body \(K\subset \mathbb{R}^{d}\),

\[ \operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ}) \ge \frac{4^{d}}{d!}.\tag{1} \]

Equality holds if and only if \(K\) is a linear image of a Hanner polytope.

For the definition of Hanner polytopes, see The equality case.

Fix a symmetric convex body \(K\subset\mathbb{R}^d\). We will construct a set \(\Sigma\subseteq K\times K^{\circ}\) and prove that \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma) \geq \frac{4^{d}}{d!}.\tag{2} \] Since \(\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(K\times K^{\circ})=\operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})\), this will imply (1).

Complexification: from \(K\) to \(\Omega\)

Define \(\lambda:[-1,1]\to[0,\infty)\) by \[ \lambda(t):=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{|t|}^{1}\operatorname{arctanh}\left(\sin\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)ds, \] and let \[ D:=\{ w\in\mathbb{C}\,\,\colon\,\,|\mathrm{Re}(w)|\lt 1,\,\,|\mathrm{Im}(w)|\lt\lambda(\mathrm{Re}(w)) \}. \]

Lemma 0
  1. The function \(\lambda\) is continuous, even, positive on \((-1,1)\), vanishes at \(\pm1\), and satisfies \(\lambda''(t)=-\sec\frac{\pi t}{2}\lt0\) on \((-1,1)\).
  2. For every \(0 \lt a\le b\), \[ \lim_{\delta \searrow0}\sup_{a\le c\le b}\Big|\frac{\lambda(1-\delta c)}{\lambda(1-\delta)}-c\Big|=0. \]
  3. The conformal map \(F\) of \(\mathbb{D}\) onto \(D\) with \(F(0)=0\) and \(F'(0)\gt0\) extends to a homeomorphism of \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) onto \(\overline D\), and pushes forward the uniform probability measure on \(\partial\mathbb D\) to the law of \(X+i\varepsilon\lambda(X)\), where \(X\) is uniform on \([-1,1]\) and \(\varepsilon\) is an independent uniform sign.

Figure 1: the domain \(D\)

Since \(\lambda\) is concave and bounded, \(D\) is a bounded convex domain, so the map \(F\) in Lemma 0 exists and is unique by the Riemann mapping theorem, and extends to \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) by Carathéodory’s theorem. Lemma 0 is proved in Appendix: Properties of the function \(F\).

Since \(K=(K^{\circ})^{\circ}\) and \(K\) is symmetric, \[ K=\{ x\in \mathbb{R}^{d}\,\,\colon\,\, \langle h,x \rangle \in [-1,1] \quad \forall\,h\in K^{\circ}\}. \] Replacing the interval \([-1,1]\) by the domain \(D\), whose intersection with \(\mathbb{R}\) is \((-1,1)\), we define \[ \Omega :=\{ z\in \mathbb{C}^{d}\,\,\colon\,\, \langle h,z \rangle \in D \quad \forall\,h\in K^{\circ}\}, \] where \(\langle h,z\rangle=\sum_jh_jz_j\). The set \(\Omega\) is convex and open, \(\Omega\cap\mathbb{R}^d=\operatorname{int}K\), and \(\mathrm{Re}\,z\in K\) for every \(z\in\overline\Omega\). For \(x\in K\), let \[ \bar\Omega_x:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^d\colon x+iy\in\overline\Omega\} \] be the fiber of \(\overline\Omega\) over \(x\). For \(y\in\mathbb{R}^d\), since \(K^{\circ}\) is symmetric and \(\lambda\) is even, \[ x+iy\in\overline\Omega\iff\langle h,y\rangle\le\lambda(\langle h,x\rangle)\quad\forall\,h\in K^{\circ}.\tag{3} \]

The set \(\Sigma\)

To motivate the definition of \(\Sigma\), note that \[ K^{\circ}=\bigcup_{y\in K}\operatorname{conv}\big(\{0\}\cup\{h\in K^{\circ}\colon\langle h,y\rangle=1\}\big).\tag{4} \] Indeed, the right-hand side is contained in \(K^\circ\), since \(K^\circ\) is convex and contains \(0\). Conversely, let \(h\in K^\circ\setminus\{0\}\) and let \(y\in K\) maximize \(\langle h,\cdot\rangle\) on \(K\). Then \(t:=\langle h,y\rangle\in(0,1]\), \(h/t\in K^{\circ}\), \(\langle h/t,y\rangle=1\), and \(h\in\operatorname{conv}\{0,h/t\}\).

For \(x\in K\), let \[ \Sigma_x:=\bigcup_{y\in\bar\Omega_x}\operatorname{conv}\big(\{0\}\cup\{h\in K^{\circ}\colon\langle h,y\rangle=\lambda(\langle h,x\rangle)\}\big)\subseteq K^{\circ},\tag{5} \] and let \[ \Sigma:=\bigcup_{x\in K}\{x\}\times\Sigma_x\subseteq K\times K^{\circ}. \] For \(z=x+iy\in\overline\Omega\) and \(h\in K^\circ\), the condition \(\langle h,y\rangle=\lambda(\langle h,x\rangle)\) in (5) says that equality holds in (3), that is, that \(\langle h,z\rangle\) lies on the upper arc \(\{t+i\lambda(t)\colon|t|\le1\}\) of \(\partial D\). Thus (5) is obtained from (4) by replacing \(K\) with \(\overline\Omega\), and the right endpoint of \([-1,1]\) with the upper arc of \(\partial D\). Note that \(\Sigma_0=K^{\circ}\) by (3) and (4).

From now on, until The equality case, we assume that \(K\) is a polytope; the general case of (2) follows by a standard approximation argument, see Lemma 5. Let \(b_1,\dots, b_{m}\) be nonzero, pairwise nonproportional vectors such that \[ K=\{ x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\,\colon\,|\langle b_{j},x \rangle| \leq 1\quad\forall\, 1\leq j\leq m \}. \] Then \(K^{\circ}=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm b_l\}\), and since \(\{h\in\mathbb{R}^d\colon\langle h,z\rangle\in D\}\) is convex and symmetric for each \(z\in\mathbb{C}^d\), \[ \Omega=\{ z\in \mathbb{C}^{d}\,\,\colon\,\, \langle b_{j},z \rangle \in D \quad \forall\,1\leq j\leq m\}, \] and likewise with \(\overline D\) and \(\overline\Omega\).

For \(J\subset[m]\), define a linear map \(B_J:\mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}^{J}\) by \[ (B_{J}x)_{j}=\langle b_{j},x \rangle ,\qquad j\in J, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}^d, \] and let \[ \mathcal{J}:=\left\{ J\in{[m]\choose d}\,\,\colon\,\,B_J\text{ is invertible} \right\} \] (note that \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(\mathbb{R}^J\) have the same dimension).

For \(J\in \mathcal{J}\) and \(\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}\), write \(\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\) for the simplex \[ \Delta_{J,\varepsilon}:=\operatorname{conv} \left( \{0\}\cup \{\varepsilon_{j}b_{j}\}_{j\in J}\right)\subset K^{\circ}, \] and, for \(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\) with \(B_Jx\in[-1,1]^J\), let \(y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\) be the unique solution \(y\in\mathbb{R}^d\) of the system \[ \langle b_j,y\rangle=\varepsilon_j\lambda(\langle b_j,x\rangle),\qquad j\in J, \] which exists since \(B_J\) is invertible. Let \[ K_{J,\varepsilon}:=\{x\in K\,\colon\,y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\in\bar\Omega_x\}. \] If \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\) and \(y=y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\), then \(\langle\varepsilon_jb_j,y\rangle=\lambda(\langle\varepsilon_jb_j,x\rangle)\) for all \(j\in J\), since \(\lambda\) is even, so \(\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\subseteq\Sigma_x\) by (5). Hence \[ \Sigma\supseteq\bigcup\limits_{J\in \mathcal{J}}\bigcup\limits_{\varepsilon \in \{-1,1\}^J}K_{J,\varepsilon}\times\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}.\tag{6} \] In fact, equality holds in (6), see Appendix: \(\Sigma\) as a union of products, but we shall not need this.

For \(x\in K\) and \(y\in\mathbb{R}^d\), the function \(h\mapsto\langle h,y\rangle-\lambda(\langle h,x\rangle)\) is convex on \(K^{\circ}\), so it suffices to check (3) at the vertices \(\pm b_l\) of \(K^{\circ}\), and since \(\lambda\) is even, \[ \bar\Omega_x=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^d\colon|\langle b_l,y\rangle|\le\lambda(\langle b_l,x\rangle)\ \ \forall\,1\leq l\leq m\}. \]

Lemma 1

For almost every \(x \in \operatorname{int} K\), the simplices \(\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\), over the pairs \((J,\varepsilon)\) with \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\), have disjoint interiors.

Proof. Let \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\cap K_{J,\varepsilon}\), let \(y:=y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\), and let \(\xi\in\operatorname{int}\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\). Then there exist \(\alpha_{j}>0\) such that \(\sum\limits_{j\in J}\alpha_{j}\lt 1\) and \[ \xi=\sum_{j\in J}\alpha_j\varepsilon_jb_j. \] For every \(y'\in\bar\Omega_x\), (3) gives \(|\langle b_j,y'\rangle|\le\lambda(\langle b_j,x\rangle)\), so \[ \langle \xi,y'\rangle\le\sum_{j\in J}\alpha_j\lambda(\langle b_j,x\rangle)=\langle\xi,y\rangle, \] with equality if and only if \(\langle b_j,y'\rangle=\varepsilon_j\lambda(\langle b_j,x\rangle)\) for all \(j\in J\), that is, if and only if \(y'=y\). So \(y\) is the unique maximizer of \(\langle\xi,\cdot\rangle\) on \(\bar\Omega_x\), that is, \(\xi\) lies in the interior of the normal cone of \(\bar\Omega_x\) at \(y\). Since the normal cones of \(\bar\Omega_x\) at distinct vertices have disjoint interiors, it suffices to show that for almost every \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\), the points \(y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\), over the pairs \((J,\varepsilon)\) with \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\), are distinct.

For fixed \(J\), since \(\lambda>0\) on \((-1,1)\), the points \(y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\) are distinct for distinct \(\varepsilon\) when \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\). Thus it suffices to consider pairs \((J,\varepsilon), (J',\varepsilon')\) with \(J\neq J'\); fix such a pair and fix \(k\in J'\setminus J\). If \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\cap K_{J',\varepsilon'}\) and \(y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)=y_{J',\varepsilon'}(x)=:y\), then \[ \langle b_k,y\rangle=\varepsilon'_k\lambda(\langle b_k,x\rangle).\tag{7} \] Write \(b_k=\sum_{j\in J}c_jb_j\) and use the coordinates \(q_j=\langle b_j,x\rangle\), \(j\in J\), on \(\operatorname{int} K\). Since \(\langle b_j,y\rangle=\varepsilon_j\lambda(q_j)\), equation (7) reads \[ G(q):=\sum_{j\in J}c_j\varepsilon_j\lambda(q_j)-\varepsilon'_k\lambda\Big(\sum_{j\in J}c_jq_j\Big)=0. \] Since \(b_k\) is not proportional to \(b_j\) for any \(j\neq k\), at least two coefficients \(c_{j_1},c_{j_2}\) are nonzero. Hence, by Lemma 0, \[ \frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial q_{j_1}\,\partial q_{j_2}}=-\varepsilon'_k c_{j_1}c_{j_2}\lambda''\Big(\sum_{j\in J}c_jq_j\Big)\neq0 \] everywhere. By the implicit function theorem, both \(\{G=0,\ \nabla G\neq0\}\) and \(\{\partial G/\partial q_{j_1}=0\}\supseteq\{\nabla G=0\}\) are locally \(C^1\) hypersurfaces, so the level set \(\{G=0\}\) has measure zero. Since there are finitely many pairs \((J,\varepsilon),(J',\varepsilon')\), this proves the lemma. \(\square\)

Each \(K_{J,\varepsilon}\) is closed, since \(\overline\Omega\) is closed and \(y_{J,\varepsilon}\) is continuous. By (6), Lemma 1 and Fubini's theorem, \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma)&\ge \int_K\sum_{(J,\varepsilon)\colon\,x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}}\operatorname{Vol}(\Delta_{J,\varepsilon})\,dx\\ &=\sum\limits_{J\in \mathcal{J}}\sum\limits_{\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}}\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(K_{J,\varepsilon}\times\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}). \end{aligned} \] The linear map \((x,h)\mapsto(B_Jx,B_J^{-\mathsf T}h)\) of \(\mathbb{R}^d\times\mathbb{R}^d\) preserves volume and maps \(K_{J,\varepsilon}\times\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\) onto \[ B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\times\operatorname{conv}\left(\{0\}\cup\{\varepsilon_je_j\}_{j\in J}\right). \] Hence, since \(\operatorname{Vol}\big(\operatorname{conv}\left(\{0\}\cup\{\varepsilon_je_j\}_{j\in J}\right)\big)=1/d!\), \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma)\ge\frac{1}{d!}\sum\limits_{J\in \mathcal{J}}\sum\limits_{\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}}\operatorname{Vol}\left(B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\right).\tag{8} \]

From \(\Omega\) to the cube to the torus

Fix \(J\in \mathcal{J}\). Since \(|\langle b_j,x\rangle|\le1\) for all \(x\in K\) and \(1\le j\le m\), \[ B_J(K)\subseteq[-1,1]^J.\tag{9} \] Define \(\Phi_J:[-1,1]^J\times\{-1,1\}^J\to(\partial D)^J\) by \[ \Phi_J(q,\varepsilon)_j:=q_{j}+i\varepsilon_{j}\lambda(q_{j}),\qquad j\in J. \] By the definition of \(y_{J,\varepsilon}\), for \(q\in[-1,1]^J\) and \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J\), \[ \Phi_J(q,\varepsilon)=q+iB_Jy_{J,\varepsilon}(B_J^{-1}q).\tag{10} \] Since \(B_J\) is injective on \(\mathbb{C}^d\), and \(\mathrm{Re}\,z\in K\) for every \(z\in\overline\Omega\), it follows that \(\Phi_J(q,\varepsilon)\in B_J(\overline\Omega)\) if and only if \(B_J^{-1}q\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\). Together with (9), this gives \[ B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})=\{q\in[-1,1]^J\,\,\colon\,\,\Phi_J(q,\varepsilon)\in B_J(\overline\Omega)\},\qquad\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J. \] Taking volumes in the last identity and summing over \(\varepsilon\) gives \[ \sum_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J}\operatorname{Vol}\big(B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\big)=\int_{[-1,1]^J}\sum_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J}\mathbf 1_{B_J(\overline\Omega)}\big(\Phi_J(q,\varepsilon)\big)\,dq.\tag{11} \] View \(F\) as a map on \(\overline{\mathbb D}^J\) by applying it coordinatewise, and for \(J\in\mathcal{J}\) let \[ A_J:=F^{-1}\big(B_J(\overline\Omega)\big)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb D}^J. \] The set \(A_J\) is closed, since \(B_J(\overline\Omega)\) is closed and \(F\) is continuous. Let \(\sigma\) be the uniform probability measure on \(\partial\mathbb D\), and let \(\mu_J:=\sigma^{\otimes J}\) be the Haar probability measure on the torus \((\partial\mathbb D)^J\). By Lemma 0, the map \((t,\varepsilon)\mapsto t+i\varepsilon\lambda(t)\) pushes the uniform probability measure on \([-1,1]\times\{-1,1\}\) forward to the image of \(\sigma\) under \(F\). Since \(F\) and \(\Phi_J\) act coordinatewise, \(\Phi_J\) pushes the uniform probability measure on \([-1,1]^J\times\{-1,1\}^J\) forward to the image of \(\mu_J\) under \(F\). Therefore the right-hand side of (11) equals \(4^d\mu_J\big(F^{-1}(B_J(\overline\Omega))\big)=4^d\mu_J(A_J)\), and (8) becomes \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma)\ge\frac{4^d}{d!}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_J(A_J). \] Thus, in order to prove (2), it suffices to prove that \[ \sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_J(A_J)\geq 1. \]

From the torus to submanifolds of the polydisc

Recap:

  • \(b_1,\dots,b_m\) are nonzero vectors spanning \(\mathbb{R}^d\).
  • \(\mathcal{J}\) is the set of \(J\in{[m]\choose d}\) for which the linear map \(B_{J}z=(\langle b_{j},z \rangle)_{j\in J}\) from \(\mathbb{C}^{d}\) to \(\mathbb{C}^{J}\) is invertible.
  • \(F:\mathbb D\to\mathbb{C}\) is an injective holomorphic map with a bounded convex image \(D\), which extends to a homeomorphism of \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) onto \(\overline D\) and satisfies \(F(0)=0\),
  • for all \(J\in \mathcal{J}\), we set \(A_J:=F^{-1}(B_J(\overline\Omega))\subseteq\overline{\mathbb D}^J\), where \(\Omega :=\{ z\in \mathbb{C}^{d}\,\,\colon\,\, \langle b_{j},z \rangle \in D \quad \forall\,1\leq j\leq m\}\),

and our goal is to prove that \[ \sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_J(A_J)\geq 1, \] where \(\mu_{J}\) is the Haar probability measure on \((\partial\mathbb D)^{J}\).

Define a linear map \(B:\mathbb{C}^d\to\mathbb{C}^m\) by \[ (Bz)_j=\langle b_j,z\rangle,\qquad 1\le j\le m,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^d, \] so that \(B_J=\pi_J\circ B\) for the coordinate projection \(\pi_J:\mathbb{C}^m\to\mathbb{C}^J\).

For \(k\ge1\) let \(p_k\) be the coordinatewise \(k\)-th power, \(p_k(v)=(v_j^k)_j\), on \(\mathbb{C}^m\) and on each \(\mathbb{C}^J\); then \(\pi_J\circ p_k=p_k\circ\pi_J\). Let \[ dV_J:=\bigwedge_{j\in J}\frac{i}{2}\,dv_j\wedge d\bar v_j \] denote the volume form of \(\mathbb{C}^J\), and let \(dV\) denote the volume form of \(\mathbb{C}^d\). For \(k\ge1\), let \(\mathbb B^{2k}_m\) and \(\mathbb B^{2k}_J\) denote the open unit balls of the \(\ell^{2k}\) norm \[ \|v\|_{2k}:=\Big(\sum_j|v_j|^{2k}\Big)^{1/2k} \] on \(\mathbb{C}^m\) and \(\mathbb{C}^J\), respectively, so that \(\pi_J(\mathbb B^{2k}_m)\subseteq\mathbb B^{2k}_J\). Denoting by \(\mathbb B_m\) the open Euclidean unit ball in \(\mathbb{C}^m\), we have \[ \mathbb B^{2k}_m=p_k^{-1}(\mathbb B_m).\tag{12} \]

For \(J\in\mathcal{J}\) and \(k\ge1\), the form \(p_k^*dV_J\) is a nonnegative multiple of \(dV_J\). Let \(\mu_{k,J}\) denote the measure on \(\overline{\mathbb D}^J\) induced by the \(2d\)-form \[ \frac{d!}{(\pi k)^d}\,\mathbf 1_{\mathbb B^{2k}_J}\,p_k^*dV_J. \] As the proof of the following lemma shows, \(\mu_{k,J}\) is the law of the point obtained from a uniformly distributed point of \(\mathbb B^2_J\) by replacing the modulus of each coordinate with its \(k\)-th root.

Lemma 2
For every \(J\in\mathcal{J}\) and \(k\ge1\), \(\mu_{k,J}\) is a probability measure, and \(\mu_{k,J}\to\mu_J\) weakly as \(k\to\infty\).

Proof. Pick a uniformly distributed point of \(\mathbb B^2_J\) and write it as \((\rho_j\omega_j)_{j\in J}\) with \(\rho_j>0\) and \(\omega_j\in\partial\mathbb D\), which is possible almost surely. Since \(\mathbb B^2_J\) and \(dV_J\) are invariant under the rotations \(v\mapsto(\zeta_jv_j)_{j\in J}\), \(\zeta\in(\partial\mathbb D)^J\), the law of \(\omega\) is a rotation-invariant probability measure on \((\partial\mathbb D)^J\), namely \(\mu_J\). We claim that \(\mu_{k,J}\) is the law of \(\rho^{1/k}\omega:=(\rho_j^{1/k}\omega_j)_{j\in J}\). This gives the lemma: \(\mu_{k,J}\) is then a probability measure, and since \(\rho^{1/k}\omega\to\omega\) almost surely as \(k\to\infty\), the laws converge weakly.

To prove the claim, note that \((\rho_j\omega_j)_{j\in J}\mapsto\rho^{1/k}\omega\) is a bijection between the points of \(\mathbb B^2_J\) with nonzero coordinates and the points of \(\mathbb B^{2k}_J\) with nonzero coordinates, and that the points with a zero coordinate form a null set. In polar coordinates \(dV_J=(2\pi)^d\prod_j\rho_j\,d\rho\otimes\mu_J\), and the substitution \(v_j=\rho_j^{1/k}\omega_j\) gives \[ p_k^*dV_J(v)=k^d(2\pi)^d\prod_j\rho_j\,d\rho\otimes\mu_J=k^d\,dV_J. \] Since the volume of \(\mathbb B^2_J\) is \(\pi^d/d!\), the law of \(\rho^{1/k}\omega\) is the measure induced by \(\frac{d!}{(\pi k)^d}\,\mathbf 1_{\mathbb B^{2k}_J}\,p_k^*dV_J\), that is, \(\mu_{k,J}\). \(\square\)

Since \(A_J\) is closed, Lemma 2 and the portmanteau theorem give \(\limsup_k\mu_{k,J}(A_J)\le\mu_J(A_J)\), and hence \[ \sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_J(A_J)\ge\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\limsup_k\mu_{k,J}(A_J)\ge\limsup_k\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_{k,J}(A_J). \] Therefore it suffices to prove that \[ \sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_{k,J}(A_J)\ge1,\qquad k\ge1.\tag{13} \]

Let \(M_{k}\) denote the \(d\)-dimensional complex submanifold \[ M_k:=\mathbb B^{2k}_m\cap F^{-1}\big(B(\Omega)\big)\subseteq\mathbb D^m. \] For \(u\in M_k\) and \(J\in\mathcal{J}\), \[ F(u)=B\,B_J^{-1}F(\pi_Ju), \] so \(\pi_J\) is injective on \(M_k\) and \(\pi_J(M_k)\subseteq A_J\cap\mathbb B^{2k}_J\).

The map \(F^{-1}\circ B\) is a biholomorphism of \(\Omega\) onto \(F^{-1}(B(\Omega))\). Let \[ f_k:=p_k\circ F^{-1}\circ B\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^m\tag{14} \] and \[ \Omega_k:=f_k^{-1}(\mathbb B_m)\subseteq\Omega. \] By (12), the map \(F^{-1}\circ B\) is a biholomorphism of \(\Omega_k\) onto \(M_k\).

We use the normalization \[ d := \partial + \bar\partial, \qquad d^{c}:=\frac{i}{4}(\bar\partial-\partial),\qquad dd^{c}=\frac{i}{2}\partial\bar\partial, \] so that \(dd^{c}|z|^{2}=\sum_{j}dx_{j}\wedge dy_{j}\) and \((dd^{c}|z|^{2})^{d}=d!\,dV\) on \(\mathbb{C}^d\); the standard facts used below are recalled in Appendix: Complex-geometric background for Lemma 3. By (A5), \((dd^c|\cdot|^2)^d=d!\sum_{J\in{[m]\choose d}}dV_J\) on \(\mathbb{C}^m\). Define a \(2d\)-form \(\sigma_{k}\) on \(M_{k}\) by \[ \sigma_k:=\frac1{d!}\,(p_k|_{M_k})^*(dd^c|\cdot|^2)^d=\sum_{J\in{[m]\choose d}}(\pi_J|_{M_k})^*\,p_k^*\,dV_J. \] Since \(F^{-1}\circ B\) is a biholomorphism of \(\Omega_k\) onto \(M_k\) and \(dd^c\) commutes with holomorphic pullbacks, the definition (14) gives \[ \int_{M_k}\sigma_k=\frac1{d!}\int_{\Omega_k}(dd^c|f_k|^2)^d.\tag{15} \] Moreover, for \(J\in{[m]\choose d}\), the pullback of \((\pi_J|_{M_k})^*p_k^*dV_J\) to \(\Omega_k\) is \((\pi_J\circ f_k)^*dV_J=|\det(Df_k)_J|^2\,dV\) by (A4), where \((Df_k)_J\) is the \(d\times d\) minor of \(Df_k\) with rows in \(J\); by (14) and the fact that \(p_k\circ F^{-1}\) acts coordinatewise, \((Df_k)_J\) is \(B_J\) multiplied on the left by a diagonal matrix, so this term vanishes for \(J\notin\mathcal{J}\). Since \(p_k^*dV_J\) is a nonnegative multiple of \(dV_J\), \[ \int_{M_k}\sigma_k=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\int_{M_k}(\pi_J|_{M_k})^*p_k^*dV_J\le\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\int_{A_J\cap \mathbb B^{2k}_J}p_k^*dV_J=\frac{(\pi k)^{d}}{d!}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_{k,J}(A_J). \] Hence (13) will follow once we prove that \[ \int_{M_{k}}\sigma_{k}\geq \frac{(\pi k)^{d}}{d!}\qquad\text{for all }k \ge 1.\tag{16} \]

A lower bound on the volume of the image of a holomorphic function

By (15), and since \(\Omega_k=\{z\in\Omega\colon|f_k(z)|\lt1\}\) by definition, (16) follows by applying the following key lemma to \(f=f_k\) and \(U=\Omega\):

Lemma 3

Theorem 7 (Lima,6 Hansen–Lima7)

Let \(\|\cdot\|\) be a norm on \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\). Suppose that every triple of points has a median in \((\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|)\). Then the unit ball of \(\|\cdot\|\) is a linear image of a Hanner polytope.

Proof. A normed space has the 3.2 intersection property if any three pairwise intersecting closed balls, of arbitrary radii, have a common point.8 Let the closed balls of radii \(\rho_i\) centered at \(x_i\), \(i=1,2,3\), intersect pairwise. Let \(m\) be a median of the centers and \(r_i=\|m-x_i\|\), so \(r_i+r_j=\|x_i-x_j\|\le\rho_i+\rho_j\). If every \(r_i\le\rho_i\), then \(m\) is a common point. Otherwise say \(t:=r_1-\rho_1>0\), so \(r_j+t\le\rho_j\) for \(j=2,3\). The point \(z=m+\frac t{r_1}(x_1-m)\) has \(\|z-x_1\|=\rho_1\) and \(\|z-x_j\|\le t+r_j\le\rho_j\). Hence the space has the 3.2 intersection property, and the conclusion is Corollary 7.4 of Hansen–Lima.9 \(\square\)

Suppose \(\operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})=4^{d}/d!\). By Lemma 6 and Theorem 7, the unit ball \(K^{\circ}\) of \(\|\cdot\|_{K^{\circ}}\) is a linear image of a Hanner polytope. Polarity swaps \(\oplus_1\) and \(\oplus_\infty\) and commutes with linear maps up to transpose-inverse, so \(K=(K^{\circ})^{\circ}\) is one too.

Conversely, for symmetric bodies \(A\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\), \(A'\subset\mathbb{R}^{l}\), the fiber of \(A\oplus_1A'\) over \(x\in A\) is \((1-\|x\|_A)A'\), so \[ \operatorname{Vol}(A\oplus_1A')=k\operatorname{Vol}(A)\operatorname{Vol}(A')\int_0^{1}t^{k-1}(1-t)^{l}\,dt=\frac{k!\,l!}{(k+l)!}\operatorname{Vol}(A)\operatorname{Vol}(A') \] and \[ \operatorname{Vol}(A\oplus_\infty A')=\operatorname{Vol}(A)\operatorname{Vol}(A'). \] Since polarity swaps the two sums, both satisfy \(\operatorname{Vol}(A\oplus A')\operatorname{Vol}\big((A\oplus A')^{\circ}\big)=\operatorname{Vol}(A)\operatorname{Vol}(A^{\circ})\cdot\operatorname{Vol}(A')\operatorname{Vol}(A'^{\circ})\big/\binom{k+l}{k}\). A segment gives \(4\), so induction gives \(4^{d}/d!\) for every Hanner polytope, and linear maps do not change the volume product. \(\square\)

The integrand \(\operatorname{arctanh}\big(\sin\frac{\pi s}{2}\big)\) is positive on \((0,1)\) and \(O\big(\log\frac{1}{1-s}\big)\) as \(s\uparrow1\), so \(\lambda\) is continuous on \([-1,1]\), \(\lambda(\pm1)=0\), and \(\lambda>0\) on \((-1,1)\). For \(0\lt |t| \lt 1\), since \(\lambda\) is even, \[ \lambda'(t)=-\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{arctanh}\Big(\sin\frac{\pi t}{2}\Big), \] and this also holds at \(t=0\), because the right-hand side is continuous and vanishes there. Differentiating again, \[ \lambda''(t)=-\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}\cos\frac{\pi t}{2}}{1-\sin^{2}\frac{\pi t}{2}}=-\sec\frac{\pi t}{2}\lt0,\qquad -1\lt t \lt 1, \] so \(\lambda\) is strictly concave on \([-1,1]\); being also even, it attains its maximum at \(0\). It follows that \(D\) is a bounded convex domain.

The series converges absolutely on \(\overline{\mathbb D}\), so \(F\) is continuous there, \(F(0)=0\), \(F'(0)=8/\pi^{2}\gt0\), \(F(\pm1)=\pm\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_j(2j+1)^{-2}=\pm1\), and \(F(\bar z)=\overline{F(z)}\). Differentiating the series and multiplying by \(z\), we get \[ zF'(z)=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\operatorname{arctanh}z, \] which extends analytically across \(\partial\mathbb D\setminus\{\pm1\}\). For \(0 \lt \theta\lt\pi\), \(\operatorname{arctanh}e^{i\theta}=\frac12\log\big(i\cot\frac\theta2\big)\), so \[ \frac{d}{d\theta}F(e^{i\theta})=\frac{8i}{\pi^{2}}\operatorname{arctanh}e^{i\theta}=-\frac{2}{\pi}+\frac{4i}{\pi^{2}}\log\cot\frac{\theta}{2}. \] Hence \(\mathrm{Re}\,F(e^{i\theta})=1-2\theta/\pi=:t\), and, by \(\log\cot\frac{x}{2}=\operatorname{arctanh}(\cos x)\), \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Im}\,F=-\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{arctanh}\big(\sin\frac{\pi t}{2}\big)=\lambda'(t)\) with \(\mathrm{Im}\,F=0\) at \(t=\pm1\).

So \(\mathrm{Im}\,F(e^{i\theta})=\lambda(t)\), and since \(F(\bar z)=\overline{F(z)}\), \[ F(e^{\pm i\theta})=t\pm i\lambda(t),\qquad t=1-\frac{2\theta}{\pi},\quad 0\le\theta\le\pi. \] Thus \(F\) maps the upper and the lower semicircle bijectively onto the upper and the lower half of \(\partial D\), and \(F|_{\partial\mathbb D}\) is a homeomorphism onto the Jordan curve \(\partial D\), which it traverses counterclockwise.

Let \(w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\partial D\). As \(r\uparrow1\), the curves \(\theta\mapsto F(re^{i\theta})\) converge uniformly to \(\theta\mapsto F(e^{i\theta})\), so for \(r\) close to \(1\) their winding number around \(w\) is \(1\) if \(w\in D\) and \(0\) if \(w\notin\overline D\). By the argument principle, \(F\) takes every value in \(D\) exactly once in \(\mathbb D\) and no value outside \(\overline D\). Since \(F(\mathbb D)\) is open, it does not meet \(\partial D\). Hence \(F\) is a biholomorphism of \(\mathbb D\) onto \(D\), and, with the boundary correspondence, a continuous bijection of the compact set \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) onto \(\overline D\), hence a homeomorphism.

Let \(\zeta\) be uniform on \(\partial\mathbb D\), and write \(\zeta=e^{i\varepsilon\theta}\) with \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}\) and \(\theta\in(0,\pi)\), which is possible almost surely. Then \(\varepsilon\) is a uniform sign, and \(\theta\) is uniform on \((0,\pi)\) and independent of \(\varepsilon\). So \(X:=1-2\theta/\pi\) is uniform on \([-1,1]\) and independent of \(\varepsilon\), and \(F(\zeta)=X+i\varepsilon\lambda(X)\).

It remains to prove the endpoint asymptotics. Substituting \(s=1-\tau\) in the definition of \(\lambda\) and using \(\operatorname{arctanh}(\cos u)=\log\cot\frac u2\), \[ \lambda(1-\eta)=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\eta}\log\cot\frac{\pi\tau}{4}\,d\tau=\frac2\pi\,\eta\Big(\log\frac1\eta+1+\log\frac4\pi\Big)+O(\eta^{3}). \] The ratio at \(\eta=\delta c\) and \(\eta=\delta\) is \(c\,\big(1+O(1/\log\tfrac1\delta)\big)\), uniformly for \(c\in[a,b]\). \(\square\)

Appendix: \(\Sigma\) as a union of products

We prove that equality holds in (6), that is, the inclusion \(\subseteq\); this is not used elsewhere in this note. Fix \(x\in K\). Call \(h\in K^{\circ}\) tight at \(x+iy\), \(y\in \bar\Omega_x\), if \(\langle h,y\rangle=\lambda(\langle h,x\rangle)\), that is, if equality holds in (3). Then \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\) if and only if \(\varepsilon_jb_j\) is tight at \(x+iy\) for all \(j\in J\), for some \(y\in\bar\Omega_x\), since \(\lambda\) is even; this \(y\) is then \(y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\). Fix \(y\in \bar\Omega_x\) and let \[\xi\in\operatorname{conv}\big(\{0\}\cup\{h\in K^{\circ}\colon h\text{ is tight at }x+iy\}\big).\]

Let \(h\in K^{\circ}\) be tight at \(x+iy\). Since \(K^{\circ}=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm b_l\}\), \(h\) is a convex combination, with positive weights, of some of the vectors \(\pm b_l\). The function \(\lambda(\langle \cdot,x\rangle)-\langle \cdot,y\rangle\) is concave on \(K^{\circ}\), nonnegative by (3), and vanishes at \(h\), so it vanishes at each of these vectors, that is, they are tight at \(x+iy\). Consequently, \[ \xi=\sum_v\beta_v\,v,\qquad\beta_v\ge0,\quad\sum_v\beta_v\le1, \] where \(v\) ranges over the vectors \(\pm b_l\) tight at \(x+iy\). Among all such representations of \(\xi\), choose one with the fewest nonzero coefficients. Then the vectors \(v\) with \(\beta_v>0\) are linearly independent.

The points \(y'\in \bar\Omega_x\) at which every \(v\) with \(\beta_v>0\) is tight, that is, \(\langle v,y'\rangle=\lambda(\langle v,x\rangle)\), form a face of \(\bar\Omega_x\) containing \(y\); let \(y'\) be a vertex of this face. Then \(y'\) is a vertex of \(\bar\Omega_x\), so the vectors \(\pm b_l\) tight at \(x+iy'\) span \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Hence the linearly independent vectors \(\{v\colon\beta_v>0\}\) can be completed, by vectors tight at \(x+iy'\), to a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^d\). A basis contains at most one of \(b_l,-b_l\), so it has the form \(\{\varepsilon_jb_j\}_{j\in J}\) with \(J\in\mathcal{J}\) and \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J\). Thus \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\), and \(\xi=\sum_v\beta_vv\in\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\). \(\square\)

The fiber over \(x\) of the union in (6) can also be seen as the image of a piecewise linear map. The polar of \(\bar\Omega_x\) is \(\bar\Omega_x^{\circ}=\operatorname{conv}\{v/\lambda(\langle v,x\rangle)\colon v\in\{\pm b_l\}\}\). A vertex \(y\) of \(\bar\Omega_x\) corresponds to the facet of \(\bar\Omega_x^{\circ}\) whose vertices are the points \(v/\lambda(\langle v,x\rangle)\) with \(v\) tight at \(x+iy\). For almost every \(x\), by the argument in the proof of Lemma 1, exactly \(d\) vectors \(v\) are tight at each vertex \(y\): for \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\) and \(y=y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\) these are \(v=\varepsilon_jb_j\), \(j\in J\). Then the map that is linear on the cone over this facet and sends \(v/\lambda(\langle v,x\rangle)\) to \(v\) stretches the cone onto \(\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\), and these maps agree on common faces of the cones. Appendix: Complex-geometric background for Lemma 3

This appendix collects the notation and the standard facts used in the proof of Lemma 3. Throughout, \(z_j=x_j+iy_j\) are the coordinates of \(\mathbb{C}^d\), and \(\mathbb{C}^d\) is oriented by \(dV=\prod_j dx_j\wedge dy_j\).

Differential forms. A smooth function \(u\) on an open set of \(\mathbb{C}^d\) has \(du=\partial u+\bar\partial u\), where \[ \partial u=\sum_j\frac{\partial u}{\partial z_j}\,dz_j,\qquad \bar\partial u=\sum_j\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bar z_j}\,d\bar z_j,\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial z_j}=\frac12\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial y_j}\Big),\quad \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar z_j}=\frac12\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial y_j}\Big). \] The operators \(\partial,\bar\partial\) extend to forms, and \(\partial^2=\bar\partial^2=0\), \(\partial\bar\partial=-\bar\partial\partial\). A function is holomorphic if and only if \(\bar\partial u=0\). For the operator \(d^c=\frac i4(\bar\partial-\partial)\) of the proof, a one-line computation gives \[ dd^c=\frac i4(\partial+\bar\partial)(\bar\partial-\partial)=\frac i2\,\partial\bar\partial,\qquad dd^cu=\frac i2\sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k}\,dz_j\wedge d\bar z_k.\tag{A1} \] The matrix \(\big(\partial^2u/\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k\big)\) is the complex Hessian (Levi matrix) of \(u\). Since \(\frac i2\,dz_j\wedge d\bar z_j=dx_j\wedge dy_j\), (A1) gives \(dd^c|z|^2=\sum_jdx_j\wedge dy_j\), and since \(d^c\) and \(d\) are real operators, \(dd^cu\) is a real \((1,1)\)-form. Taking the \(d\)-th exterior power in (A1) gives, for smooth \(u\), \[ (dd^cu)^d=d!\,\det\Big(\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k}\Big)\,dV.\tag{A2} \] For \(u=|z|^2\) the matrix is the identity, which gives \((dd^c|z|^2)^d=d!\,dV\). In particular, if \(r\mathbb{B}_d\) is the ball of radius \(r\) in \(\mathbb{C}^d\), whose volume is \(\pi^dr^{2d}/d!\), then \[ \int_{r\mathbb{B}_d}(dd^c|z|^2)^d=(\pi r^2)^d.\tag{A3} \]

Pullbacks. If \(\Phi\) is a holomorphic map between open subsets of complex Euclidean spaces, then pullback by \(\Phi\) commutes with \(\partial\) and \(\bar\partial\), hence with \(d\), \(d^c\) and \(dd^c\). In particular \(dd^c|f|^2=f^*(dd^c|w|^2)\) for holomorphic \(f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}^m\). Moreover, a holomorphic map \(\Phi\colon\mathbb{C}^d\supseteq U\to\mathbb{C}^d\) has real Jacobian determinant \(|\det D\Phi|^2\), where \(D\Phi\) is the complex Jacobian matrix, so \[ \Phi^*dV=|\det D\Phi|^2\,dV.\tag{A4} \] Finally, write \(dV_J=\bigwedge_{j\in J}\frac i2dw_j\wedge d\bar w_j\) for the volume form of \(\mathbb{C}^J\), where \(J\subseteq[m]\), \(|J|=d\). Expanding \((dd^c|w|^2)^d=\big(\sum_{j=1}^m\frac i2dw_j\wedge d\bar w_j\big)^d\), each of the \(2\)-forms \(\frac i2dw_j\wedge d\bar w_j\) commutes with the others and squares to zero, so only products of \(d\) distinct ones survive, and each such product occurs \(d!\) times: \[ (dd^c|w|^2)^d=d!\sum_{J\in{[m]\choose d}}dV_J\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{C}^m.\tag{A5} \] Combining (A4) and (A5) with \(\pi_J\circ f\) in place of \(\Phi\) gives \((dd^c|f|^2)^d=d!\sum_J|\det(Df)_J|^2\,dV\) for holomorphic \(f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}^m\).

Plurisubharmonic functions. A smooth real function \(u\) on an open set of \(\mathbb{C}^d\) is plurisubharmonic (psh) if its complex Hessian is positive semidefinite at every point; equivalently, \(\sum u_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k\ge0\) for all \(\xi\in\mathbb{C}^d\), that is, the restriction of \(u\) to every complex line is subharmonic. Facts used:

  1. (Positivity.) If \(u\) is smooth and psh, then \(\det(u_{j\bar k})\ge0\), so \((dd^cu)^d\ge0\) by (A2).
  2. (Holomorphic images.) If \(f\) is holomorphic and nonvanishing on an open set, then \(\log|f|^2\) is smooth and psh there: for a vector \(\xi\), writing \(a=Df\,\xi\), its Levi form is \[ \frac{|f|^2|a|^2-|\langle a,f\rangle|^2}{|f|^4}\ \ge0 \] by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Also \(|f|^2\) is psh (its Levi form is \(|a|^2\)), and \(c\log|z|^2+\text{const}\) is psh on \(\mathbb{C}^d\setminus\{0\}\) for \(c\ge0\), as the case \(f(z)=z\).
  3. (Convex functions of psh functions.) If \(u_1,u_2\) are smooth and psh and \(\chi\colon\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\) is smooth, convex, and nondecreasing in each variable, then \(\chi(u_1,u_2)\) is smooth and psh. Indeed, by the chain rule its complex Hessian is \(\sum_a\chi_a\,\mathrm{Hess}_{\mathbb{C}}u_a+\sum_{a,b}\chi_{ab}\,\partial u_a\otimes\bar\partial u_b\), where \(\chi_a\ge0\) by monotonicity and \((\chi_{ab})\ge0\) by convexity.
  4. (Regularized maximum.) The only property of \(\widetilde{\max}\) used in the proof is that \(\widetilde{\max}(x,y)=\max(x,y)\) when \(|x-y|\ge1\); so \(\widetilde{\max}(g,h)=g\) where \(g\ge h+1\), and \(=h\) where \(h\ge g+1\). Such a function exists (convolve \(\max\) with a smooth nonnegative bump of small radius, adding a suitable constant if needed to keep it nondecreasing and convex; see the footnote at the end of the proof of Lemma 3).

Stokes' theorem and regular values. Let \(f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}^m\) be smooth, and let \(s>0\). If \(s\) is a regular value of \(|f|\), meaning that \(d|f|\neq0\) at every point of \(\{|f|=s\}\), then \(\{|f|\le s\}\) is a manifold with boundary \(\partial U_s=\{|f|=s\}\), which is compact when \(U_s\) is relatively compact in \(U\) and \(\{|f|\le s\}\) is closed in \(U\). By Sard's theorem, almost every \(s\) is a regular value. Boundaries carry the orientation induced from \(\mathbb{C}^d\) (outward normal first), so for a smooth \((2d-1)\)-form \(\beta\) on a neighborhood of \(\overline{U_s}\), \[ \int_{U_s}d\beta=\int_{\partial U_s}\beta.\tag{A6} \] The same holds on the region \(U_s\setminus r\overline{\mathbb{B}}_d\) between two such boundaries, whose oriented boundary is \(\partial U_s\) minus \(\partial(r\mathbb{B}_d)\), where \(\partial(r\mathbb{B}_d)\) is oriented as the boundary of the ball. This is how (A6) is used to compare the two boundary integrals in the proof. Two further remarks about forms restricted to a hypersurface \(\{\varrho=\text{const}\}\): the restriction of \(d\varrho\) to the hypersurface vanishes, so \[ \big(d\log\varrho\wedge d^c\log\varrho\big)\big|_{\{\varrho=\text{const}\}}=0,\tag{A7} \] which is why \(dd^c\log|f|^2\) and \(|f|^{-2}dd^c|f|^2\) agree on \(\partial U_s\) (take \(\varrho=|f|^2\)) and likewise \(dd^c\log|z|^2=r^{-2}dd^c|z|^2\) on the sphere \(\partial(r\mathbb{B}_d)\); and, by (A6) applied to the ball and (A3), \[ \int_{\partial(r\mathbb{B}_d)}d^c|z|^2\wedge(dd^c|z|^2)^{d-1}=\int_{r\mathbb{B}_d}(dd^c|z|^2)^d=(\pi r^2)^d.\tag{A8} \] Here we used \(d\big(d^c|z|^2\wedge(dd^c|z|^2)^{d-1}\big)=(dd^c|z|^2)^d\), valid because \(dd^c\) of anything is closed.

Other notation. \(\mathbb{B}_m\) denotes the open Euclidean unit ball of \(\mathbb{C}^m\); \(|\cdot|\) is the Hermitian norm; \(\overline{U_s}\) is the closure; \(g=O(1)\) means that \(g\) is bounded near the point in question; and \(Df\) is the complex Jacobian of a holomorphic map \(f\), with \((Df)_J\) its \(d\times d\) minor with rows in \(J\).
