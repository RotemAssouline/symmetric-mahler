Statement

A convex body is a compact convex set \(K\subset\mathbb{R}^d\) with nonempty interior; it is symmetric if \(K=-K\). Recall that the polar body \(K^\circ\) of a convex body \(K\) containing the origin in its interior is defined by \[K^{\circ}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^d\colon\langle x,y\rangle\le1\ \ \forall\,x\in K\}.\]

Theorem

For every symmetric convex body \(K\subset \mathbb{R}^{d}\),

\[ \operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ}) \ge \frac{4^{d}}{d!}.\tag{1} \]

Equality holds if and only if \(K\) is a linear image of a Hanner polytope.

For the definition of Hanner polytopes, see The equality case.

Complexification: from \(K\) to \(\Omega\)

Define \(\lambda:[-1,1]\to[0,\infty)\) by \[ \lambda(t):=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{|t|}^{1}\operatorname{arctanh}\left(\sin\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)ds, \] and let \[ D:=\{ w\in\mathbb{C}\,\,\colon\,\,|\mathrm{Re}(w)|\lt 1,\,\,|\mathrm{Im}(w)|\lt\lambda(\mathrm{Re}(w)) \}. \]

Lemma 0
  1. The function \(\lambda\) is continuous, even, positive on \((-1,1)\), vanishes at \(\pm1\), and satisfies \(\lambda''(t)=-\sec\frac{\pi t}{2}\lt0\) on \((-1,1)\).
  2. As \(\delta\searrow0\), \[ \lambda(1-\delta)=\frac2\pi\,\delta\log\frac1\delta+O(\delta). \]
  3. The conformal map \(F\) of the unit disc \(\mathbb{D}\) onto \(D\) with \(F(0)=0\) and \(F'(0)\gt0\) extends to a homeomorphism of \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) onto \(\overline D\), and satisfies \(F\big(e^{i\varepsilon\pi(1-t)/2}\big)=t+i\varepsilon\lambda(t)\) for \(t\in[-1,1]\) and \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}\).
Figure 1: the domain \(D\)

Since \(\lambda\) is concave and bounded, \(D\) is a bounded convex domain, so the map \(F\) in Lemma 0 exists and is unique by the Riemann mapping theorem, and extends to \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) by Carathéodory’s theorem. Lemma 0 is proved in Appendix: Properties of the domain \(D\) and where they are used, which also lists where in the proof each property of \(D\) is used.

Until The equality case, we assume that \(K\) is a polytope; the general case of (1) follows by a standard approximation argument. Let \(b_1,\dots, b_{m}\) be nonzero, pairwise nonproportional vectors such that \[ K=\{ x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\,\colon\,|\langle b_{j},x \rangle| \leq 1\quad\forall\, 1\leq j\leq m \}. \] Then \(K^{\circ}=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm b_j\}\). Replacing the interval \([-1,1]\) by the domain \(D\), whose intersection with \(\mathbb{R}\) is \((-1,1)\), we define \[ \Omega :=\{ z\in \mathbb{C}^{d}\,\,\colon\,\, \langle b_{j},z \rangle \in D \quad \forall\,1\leq j\leq m\}. \] The set \(\Omega\) is convex and open, \(\Omega\cap\mathbb{R}^d=\operatorname{int}K\), and \(\mathrm{Re}\,z\in K\) for every \(z\in\overline\Omega\). Since \(\Omega\) is convex and contains \(0\), its closure is \(\{z\colon\langle b_j,z\rangle\in\overline D\ \forall\,j\}\), and \(\overline D=\{w\colon|\mathrm{Re}\,w|\le1,\ |\mathrm{Im}\,w|\le\lambda(\mathrm{Re}\,w)\}\) since \(\lambda\) is continuous and vanishes at \(\pm1\). Hence \[ x+iy\in\overline\Omega\iff|\langle b_j,y\rangle|\le\lambda(\langle b_j,x\rangle)\quad\forall\,1\leq j\leq m.\tag{2} \]

For \(J\subseteq[m] := \{1,\dots,m\}\), define a linear map \(B_J:\mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}^{J}\) by \[ (B_{J}x)_{j}=\langle b_{j},x \rangle ,\qquad j\in J, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}^d, \] and let \[ \mathcal{J}:=\left\{ J\in{[m]\choose d}\,\,\colon\,\,B_J\text{ is invertible} \right\} \] (note that \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(\mathbb{R}^J\) have the same dimension). Define also \(B:\mathbb{C}^d\to\mathbb{C}^m\) by \[ (Bz)_j=\langle b_j,z\rangle,\qquad 1\le j\le m,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^d, \] so that \(B_J=\pi_J\circ B\) where \(\pi_J:\mathbb{C}^m\to\mathbb{C}^J\) is the canonical projection.

Note that, since \(|\langle b_j,x\rangle|\le1\) for all \(x\in K\) and \(1\le j\le m\), \[ B_J(K)\subseteq[-1,1]^J, \qquad J\in \mathcal{J}.\tag{3} \]

For \(J\in\mathcal{J}\), \(\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}\) and \(x\in B_J^{-1}([-1,1]^J)\), let \(y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\) be the unique solution to \[ \langle b_j,y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\rangle=\varepsilon_j\lambda(\langle b_j,x\rangle),\qquad j\in J, \] which exists since \(B_J\) is invertible. For \(x\in K\), let \[ \overline\Omega_x:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^d\,\,\colon\,\, x+iy\in\overline\Omega\right\} \] be the fiber of \(\overline\Omega\) over \(x\), and define \[ K_{J,\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in K\,\,\colon\,\,y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\in\overline\Omega_x\right\}. \] If \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\), then \(y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\) is the vertex of \(\overline\Omega_x\) at which the inequalities in (2) for \(j\in J\) are equalities.

Lemma 1

The sets \[ K_{J,\varepsilon}\times\Delta_{J,\varepsilon},\qquad\qquad J\in\mathcal{J},\ \varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J, \] have pairwise null intersections.

Proof.

Let \((J,\varepsilon)\neq(J',\varepsilon')\). Since two simplices with disjoint interiors intersect in a null set, we may assume that there is some \(\xi\in\operatorname{int}\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\cap\operatorname{int}\Delta_{J',\varepsilon'}\), and then it suffices to show that \(K_{J,\varepsilon}\cap K_{J',\varepsilon'}\) is null. Let \(\alpha_{j}>0\) be such that \(\sum\limits_{j\in J}\alpha_{j}\lt 1\) and \[ \xi=\sum_{j\in J}\alpha_j\varepsilon_jb_j. \] Let \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\cap K_{J,\varepsilon}\) and \(y:=y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\).

By (2) and the definition of \(\overline\Omega_x\), we have \[ \langle \xi,y'\rangle\le\sum_{j\in J}\alpha_j\lambda(\langle b_j,x\rangle)=\langle\xi,y\rangle, \qquad y' \in \overline\Omega_x, \] with equality if and only if \(\langle b_j,y'\rangle=\varepsilon_j\lambda(\langle b_j,x\rangle)\) for all \(j\in J\), that is, if and only if \(y'=y\). So \(y\) is the unique maximizer of \(\langle\xi,\cdot\rangle\) on \(\overline\Omega_x\), that is, \(\xi\) lies in the interior of the normal cone of \(\overline\Omega_x\) at \(y\). Since the normal cones of \(\overline\Omega_x\) at distinct points have disjoint interiors, \[ y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)=y_{J',\varepsilon'}(x)\tag{4} \] whenever \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\cap K_{J,\varepsilon}\cap K_{J',\varepsilon'}\). Thus it suffices to prove that (4) fails for almost every \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\).

If \(J=J'\), then \(\varepsilon\ne\varepsilon'\), and since \(\lambda>0\) on \((-1,1)\), (4) never holds on \(\operatorname{int}K\). So let \(J\neq J'\), and fix \(l\in J'\setminus J\). Write \(b_l=\sum_{j\in J}c_jb_j\) and use the coordinates \(q_j=\langle b_j,x\rangle\), \(j\in J\), on \(\operatorname{int} K\). If \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\) satisfies (4), then by the definitions of \(y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\) and \(y_{J',\varepsilon'}(x)\), \[ G(q):=\sum_{j\in J}c_j\varepsilon_j\lambda(q_j)-\varepsilon'_l\lambda\Big(\sum_{j\in J}c_jq_j\Big)=\langle b_l,y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)-y_{J',\varepsilon'}(x)\rangle=0. \] Since \(b_l\) is not proportional to \(b_j\) for any \(j\neq l\), at least two coefficients \(c_{j_1},c_{j_2}\) are nonzero. Hence, by Lemma 0, \[ \frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial q_{j_1}\,\partial q_{j_2}}=-\varepsilon'_l c_{j_1}c_{j_2}\lambda''\Big(\sum_{j\in J}c_jq_j\Big)\neq0 \] everywhere. By the implicit function theorem, both \(\{G=0,\ \nabla G\neq0\}\) and \(\{\partial G/\partial q_{j_1}=0\}\supseteq\{\nabla G=0\}\) are locally \(C^1\) hypersurfaces, so the level set \(\{G=0\}\) has measure zero. \(\square\)

For \(J\in\mathcal{J}\), the linear map \((x,h)\mapsto\big(B_Jx,(B_J^{\mathsf T})^{-1}h\big)\) from \(\mathbb{R}^d\times\mathbb{R}^d\) to \(\mathbb{R}^J\times\mathbb{R}^J\) preserves volume, and \((B_J^{\mathsf T})^{-1}b_j=e_j\) for \(j\in J\), where \((e_j)_{j\in J}\) is the standard basis of \(\mathbb{R}^J\). Since the simplex \(\operatorname{conv}\big(\{0\}\cup\{\varepsilon_je_j\}_{j\in J}\big)\) has volume \(1/d!\) for every \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J\), it follows that \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{J\in \mathcal{J}}\sum_{\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}}\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(K_{J,\varepsilon}\times\Delta_{J,\varepsilon})&=\sum_{J\in \mathcal{J}}\sum_{\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}}\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}\Big(B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\times(B_J^{\mathsf T})^{-1}(\Delta_{J,\varepsilon})\Big)\\ &=\sum_{J\in \mathcal{J}}\sum_{\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}}\operatorname{Vol}\big(B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\big)\operatorname{Vol}\Big(\operatorname{conv}\big(\{0\}\cup\{\varepsilon_je_j\}_{j\in J}\big)\Big)\\ &=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{J\in \mathcal{J}}\sum_{\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}}\operatorname{Vol}\big(B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\big). \end{aligned}\tag{5} \] Since the sets \(K_{J,\varepsilon}\times\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\) are contained in \(K\times K^{\circ}\) and have pairwise null intersections by Lemma 1, it follows that \[ \operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})\ge\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{J\in \mathcal{J}}\sum_{\varepsilon \in \{ -1,1 \}^{J}}\operatorname{Vol}\big(B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\big).\tag{6} \]

From \(K\) to the cube to the torus

Here and below, we view \(F\) and \(F^{-1}\) as maps on \(\overline{\mathbb D}^m\) and \(\overline D^m\) respectively (and similarly on \(\overline{\mathbb D}^J\) and \(\overline D^J\)), by letting them act coordinatewise. For \(J\in\mathcal{J}\), let \[ A_J:=F^{-1}\big(B_J(\overline\Omega)\big)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb D}^J. \]

Define \(\Phi:[-1,1]\times\{-1,1\}\to\partial\mathbb D\) by \[ \Phi(t,\varepsilon):=e^{i\varepsilon\pi(1-t)/2}, \] so that, by Lemma 0, \[ F\big(\Phi(t,\varepsilon)\big)=t+i\varepsilon\lambda(t),\qquad t\in[-1,1],\ \varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}.\tag{7} \]

Figure 4: the map \(\Phi\)

View \(\Phi\) as a map on \([-1,1]^J\times\{-1,1\}^J\) by applying it coordinatewise. By (7) and the definition of \(y_{J,\varepsilon}\), for \(q\in[-1,1]^J\) and \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J\), \[ F\big(\Phi(q,\varepsilon)\big)=B_J\big(B_J^{-1}q+iy_{J,\varepsilon}(B_J^{-1}q)\big). \] Since \(B_J\) is injective on \(\mathbb{C}^d\), and \(\mathrm{Re}\,z\in K\) for every \(z\in\overline\Omega\), it follows that \(F(\Phi(q,\varepsilon))\in B_J(\overline\Omega)\) if and only if \(q\in B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\). Thus, by (3) and the definition of \(A_J\), \[ \Phi^{-1}(A_J)=\bigcup_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J}B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\times\{\varepsilon\}.\tag{8} \] Let \(\mu_J\) be the Haar probability measure on the torus \((\partial\mathbb D)^J\), which we view also as a measure on the polydisc \(\overline{\mathbb D}^J\) by pushing it forward via the inclusion map. The map \(\Phi\) pushes forward the uniform probability measure on \([-1,1]^J\times\{-1,1\}^J\) to \(\mu_J\). Therefore, by (8), \[ \sum_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^J}\operatorname{Vol}\big(B_J(K_{J,\varepsilon})\big)=4^d\mu_J(A_J).\tag{9} \]

Figure 5: from the torus to the cube to \(K\)

By (6) and (9), \[ \operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})\ge\frac{4^d}{d!}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_J(A_J).\tag{10} \] Thus it remains to prove that \[ \sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_J(A_J)\ge1.\tag{11} \]

From the torus to the polydisc to \(\Omega\)

Write \[ M:=F^{-1}\big(B(\Omega)\big)\subseteq\mathbb D^m. \] Since the vectors \(b_1,\dots,b_m\) span \(\mathbb{R}^d\), and \(\langle b_j,z\rangle\in D\) for all \(z \in \Omega\) and all \(j \in [m]\), the map \(F^{-1}\circ B\) is a biholomorphism of \(\Omega\) onto the \(d\)-dimensional complex submanifold \(M\). Since \(F\) is a homeomorphism of \(\overline{\mathbb D}^m\) onto \(\overline D^m\) and \(B\) is injective, we have \(\overline M=F^{-1}\big(B(\overline\Omega)\big)\), and therefore \[A_J=\pi_J(\overline M), \qquad J\in\mathcal{J}.\]

In order to prove (11), we shall approximate each measure \(\mu_J\) by a sequence of smooth volume forms on the polydisc, and the manifold \(M\) by a sequence \(M_k\) of complex submanifolds of the polydisc. Inequality (11) will then be reduced to a complex-analytic lemma.

For \(k\ge1\) let \(p_k\) be the coordinatewise \(k\)-th power \[p_k(v)_j=v_j^k, \qquad 1 \le j \le m\] on \(\mathbb{C}^m\) and on each \(\mathbb{C}^J\). Let \[ dV_J:=\bigwedge_{j\in J}\frac{i}{2}\,dv_j\wedge d\bar v_j \] denote the volume form of \(\mathbb{C}^J\), and let \(dV\) denote the volume form of \(\mathbb{C}^d\). Let \(\mathbb B^{2k}_m\) and \(\mathbb B^{2k}_J\) denote the open unit \(\ell^{2k}\)-balls \[ \|v\|_{2k}:=\Big(\sum_j|v_j|^{2k}\Big)^{1/2k} \] on \(\mathbb{C}^m\) and \(\mathbb{C}^J\), respectively. Note that \[ \mathbb B^{2k}_m=p_k^{-1}(\mathbb B^2_m), \qquad k \ge 1.\tag{12} \]

Fix \(J\in\mathcal{J}\). Every point in \(\mathbb B^2_J\) with nonvanishing coordinates can be written as \(\rho\omega=(\rho_j\omega_j)_{j\in J}\) with \(\rho_j>0\) and \(\omega_j\in\partial\mathbb D\); write \(\rho^{1/k}\omega:=(\rho_j^{1/k}\omega_j)_{j\in J}\). The map \(\rho\omega\mapsto\rho^{1/k}\omega\) extends to a homeomorphism of \(\mathbb B^2_J\) onto \(\mathbb B^{2k}_J\), and is an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism away from the union of the coordinate hyperplanes. Writing \(\omega_j=e^{i\theta_j}\), the Euclidean volume form can be written as \(dV_J=\bigwedge_j\rho_j\,d\rho_j\wedge d\theta_j\), and in these coordinates the inverse map \(\rho\omega\mapsto\rho^k\omega\) is \((\rho,\theta)\mapsto(\rho^k,\theta)\), while \(p_k\) is \((\rho,\theta)\mapsto(\rho^k,k\theta)\). Since \((\rho,\theta)\mapsto(\rho,k\theta)\) pulls \(dV_J\) back to \(k^d\,dV_J\), the pullback of \(dV_J\) under \(\rho\omega\mapsto\rho^k\omega\) is \(k^{-d}\,p_k^*dV_J\). Since the volume of \(\mathbb B^2_J\) is \(\pi^d/d!\), it follows that, if \(\rho\omega\) is a uniformly distributed random point of \(\mathbb B^2_J\), then \[ \mathbb P\big(\rho^{1/k}\omega\in A_J\big)=\frac{d!}{\pi^d}\operatorname{Vol}\big\{\rho\omega\in\mathbb B^2_J\colon\rho^{1/k}\omega\in A_J\big\}=\frac{d!}{(\pi k)^d}\int_{A_J\cap\mathbb B^{2k}_J}p_k^*dV_J. \] Note that \(\omega\) is distributed according to the Haar measure \(\mu_J\). The set \(A_J=\pi_J(\overline M)\) is compact, and \(\rho^{1/k}\omega\to\omega\) as \(k\to\infty\), so \(\limsup_k\mathbf 1_{A_J}(\rho^{1/k}\omega)\le\mathbf 1_{A_J}(\omega)\). Hence, by the reverse Fatou lemma, \[ \sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_J(A_J)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbb P\big(\omega\in A_J\big)\ge\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbb P\big(\rho^{1/k}\omega\in A_J\big)=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{d!}{(\pi k)^d}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\int_{A_J\cap\mathbb B^{2k}_J}p_k^*dV_J. \]

For \(k \ge 1\), define \[ M_k:=M\cap\mathbb B^{2k}_m. \] For \(J\in\mathcal{J}\), the map \(\pi_J\) is a biholomorphism of \(M\) onto the open set \(F^{-1}\big(B_J(\Omega)\big)\), with inverse \(F^{-1}\circ B\circ B_J^{-1}\circ F\). Moreover, \[\pi_J(M_k)\subseteq\pi_J(\overline M)\cap\pi_J(\mathbb B^{2k}_m)\subseteq A_J\cap\mathbb B^{2k}_J,\] and \(p_k^*dV_J=|\det Dp_k|^2\,dV_J\) by (A4). Hence \[ \int_{M_k}(\pi_J|_{M_k})^*p_k^*dV_J=\int_{\pi_J(M_k)}p_k^*dV_J\le\int_{A_J\cap\mathbb B^{2k}_J}p_k^*dV_J,\qquad J\in\mathcal{J}. \] Thus (11) would follow if we prove that \[ \sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\int_{M_k}(\pi_J|_{M_k})^*p_k^*dV_J\ge\frac{(\pi k)^d}{d!}\qquad\text{for all }k\ge1.\tag{13} \]

Define \[ f_k:=p_k\circ F^{-1}\circ B:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^m.\tag{14} \] By (12), the map \(F^{-1}\circ B\) is a biholomorphism from the open set \[ \Omega_k:=f_k^{-1}(\mathbb B^2_m)\subseteq\Omega \] onto \(M_k\). Define a \(2d\)-form \(\sigma_{k}\) on \(M_{k}\) by \[ \sigma_k:=\frac1{d!}\,(p_k|_{M_k})^*(dd^c|\cdot|^2)^d=\sum_{J\in{[m]\choose d}}(\pi_J|_{M_k})^*\,p_k^*\,dV_J, \] where \(d^c:=\frac i4(\bar\partial-\partial)\), and the second equality follows from (A5) (or the Cauchy–Binet formula); see Appendix: Complex-geometric background. Thus \(\int_{M_k}\sigma_k\) is the \(2d\)-dimensional volume of \(p_k(M_k)\), counted with multiplicity, and the \(J\)-th term is the volume of its projection \(\pi_J(p_k(M_k))\), counted with multiplicity.

Let \(J\in{[m]\choose d}\). The map \(\pi_J\circ f_k=p_k\circ F^{-1}\circ B_J\) pulls the \(J\)-th term of \(\sigma_k\) back to \(\Omega_k\): \[ (F^{-1}\circ B)^*(\pi_J|_{M_k})^*p_k^*dV_J=(\pi_J\circ f_k)^*dV_J\overset{\href{#eq-A4}{(A4)}}{=}\big|\det D(\pi_J\circ f_k)\big|^2\,dV\qquad\text{on }\Omega_k. \] Since \(p_k\circ F^{-1}\) acts coordinatewise, \(D(\pi_J\circ f_k)\) is \(B_J\) multiplied on the left by a diagonal matrix, so this term vanishes for \(J\notin\mathcal{J}\). Hence \(\int_{M_k}\sigma_k\) is the left-hand side of (13). Moreover, \[ \begin{aligned} \int_{M_k}\sigma_k&=\int_{\Omega_k}(F^{-1}\circ B)^*\sigma_k=\frac1{d!}\int_{\Omega_k}f_k^*(dd^c|\cdot|^2)^d\\ &=\frac1{d!}\int_{\Omega_k}(dd^c|f_k|^2)^d, \end{aligned}\tag{15} \] where the first equality is the change of variables formula, since \(F^{-1}\circ B\) is a biholomorphism, and hence an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism, of \(\Omega_k\) onto \(M_k\); the second follows from the definition (14) of \(f_k\); and the third from the fact that \(dd^c\) commutes with holomorphic pullbacks. Thus (13) would follow if we prove that \[ \int_{\Omega_k}(dd^c|f_k|^2)^d\ge(\pi k)^d\qquad\text{for all }k\ge1. \] Since \(\Omega_k=\{|f_k|\lt1\}\), this is the content of the following lemma, applied to \(f=f_k\).

Lemma 2

Let \(d,m\geq 1\). Let \(\Omega\subset \mathbb{C}^{d}\) be an open set containing the origin, and let \(f:\Omega\to \mathbb{C}^{m}\) be a holomorphic function satisfying

  1. \(f^{-1}(0)=\{ 0 \}\)
  2. \(|f(z)|\le C|z|^{k}\) on a neighbourhood of \(0\), for some integer \(k\geq 1\) and some \(C>0\),
  3. for every \(s \lt 1\) the sublevel set \(\Omega(s):=\{ |f|\lt s \}\) is relatively compact in \(\Omega\).

Then \[ \int_{\Omega(1)}(dd^{c}|f|^{2})^{d}\geq(\pi k)^{d}. \]

Geometrically, Lemma 2 says that the volume of \(f(\Omega(1))\), counted with multiplicity, is at least that of the ball of radius \(\sqrt k\) in \(\mathbb{C}^d\). This is not used in the proof.

Let us verify that the hypotheses of Lemma 2 are satisfied with \(f=f_k\). The set \(\Omega\) is open and contains \(0\), and \(f_k\) is holomorphic on \(\Omega\) because \(B(\Omega)\subseteq D^m\) and \(F^{-1}\) acts coordinatewise. Since \(F(0)=0\) and \(F\) is injective, \(f_k(z)=0\) if and only if \(Bz=0\), that is, \(z=0\).

Since \(F^{-1}\) is holomorphic near \(0\) and \(F^{-1}(0)=0\), there are \(\delta>0\) and \(C>0\) with \(|F^{-1}(w)|\le C|w|\) for \(|w|\lt\delta\). Since \(|f_k(z)|^{2}=\sum_{j}\big|F^{-1}(\langle b_j,z\rangle)\big|^{2k}\), for \(z\) near \(0\) we have \[ |f_k(z)|\le\sqrt m\,C^{k}\max_j|\langle b_j,z\rangle|^{k}. \] Since \(B\) is injective, \(\max_j|\langle b_j,\cdot\rangle|\) is a norm on \(\mathbb{C}^d\), so \(|f_k(z)|\le C'|z|^{k}\) for some \(C'>0\) near the origin.

Let \(0\lt s \lt1\). For \(z\in \Omega(s)\) and every \(j\), \[|F^{-1}(\langle b_j,z\rangle)|^{k}\le|f_k(z)|\lt s,\] so \(\langle b_j,z\rangle\in F(s^{1/k}\overline{\mathbb D})\). Hence \[ \overline{\Omega(s)}\subseteq\{z\in\mathbb{C}^d\colon\langle b_j,z\rangle\in F(s^{1/k}\overline{\mathbb D})\ \ \forall\,1\le j\le m\}=B^{-1}\big(F(s^{1/k}\overline{\mathbb D})^{m}\big). \] The set \(F(s^{1/k}\overline{\mathbb D})\) is a compact subset of \(D\) and \(B\) is injective, so the right-hand side is a compact subset of \(\Omega\). Thus \(\Omega(s)\) is relatively compact in \(\Omega\).

Hence Lemma 2 applies to \(f=f_k\), which proves (13), and therefore (11). By (10), this concludes the proof of (1), given Lemma 2.

Proof of Lemma 2.

(The notation and standard facts used here are recalled in Appendix: Complex-geometric background.) Let \(s \in(0,1)\) be a regular value of \(|f|\). By Stokes' theorem, \[ \int_{\Omega(s)}(dd^{c}|f|^{2})^{d}=\int_{\partial\Omega(s)}d^{c}|f|^{2}\wedge(dd^{c}|f|^{2})^{d-1}. \] Write \[ \gamma:=d^{c}\log|f|^{2}=|f|^{-2}d^{c}|f|^{2}, \] and note that \[ d\gamma=dd^{c}\log|f|^{2}=|f|^{-2}dd^{c}|f|^{2}-|f|^{-4}d|f|^{2}\wedge d^{c}|f|^{2}. \] Since \(|f|\equiv s\) on \(\partial\Omega(s)\), by (A7) with \(\varrho=|f|^2\) we have \[ \gamma=s^{-2}d^{c}|f|^{2}\qquad \text{and}\qquad d\gamma=s^{-2}dd^{c}|f|^{2}\qquad \text{on $\partial\Omega(s)$.} \] Hence \[ \int_{\Omega(s)}(dd^{c}|f|^{2})^{d}=s^{2d}\int_{\partial\Omega(s)}\gamma \wedge(d\gamma)^{d-1}.\tag{16} \] The function \(\log|f|^{2}\) is smooth and plurisubharmonic on \(\Omega(s)\setminus\{0\}\) by the first assumption.

For \(0 \lt \eta\lt 1\) set \[ g:=\log\frac{|f|^{2}}{s^{2}}\qquad \text{and}\qquad h:=(1-\eta)k\log\frac{|z|^{2}}{\max_{\overline{\Omega(s)}}|z|^{2}}-2 . \] On \(\overline{\Omega(s)}\) we have \(h\le-2\), while \(g=0\) on \(\partial\Omega(s)\). Hence \(g>h+1\) near \(\partial\Omega(s)\).

By the second assumption \(g\le k\log|z|^{2}+O(1)\) near the origin, so \[ h-g\ge-\eta k\log|z|^{2}+O(1)\to\infty \qquad \text{as $z\to 0$.} \] Let \(r>0\) be small enough that \(r\overline{\mathbb B}_d\subset \Omega(s)\) and \(h>g+1\) near \(\partial(r\mathbb B_d)\), where \(\mathbb B_d\) is the open unit ball of \(\mathbb{C}^d\). Let \(\widetilde{\max}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}\) be smooth, convex and nondecreasing in each variable, with \(\widetilde{\max}(x,y)=\max(x,y)\) whenever \(|x-y|\geq 1\). Then the function \(\widetilde{\max}(g,h)\) is smooth and plurisubharmonic on \(\Omega(s)\setminus r\overline{\mathbb B}_d\), and equals \(g\) near \(\partial\Omega(s)\) and \(h\) near \(\partial(r\mathbb B_d)\). Hence, since \(d^{c}g=\gamma\) and \((dd^{c}\,\widetilde{\max}(g,h))^{d}\ge0\) on \(\Omega(s)\setminus r\overline{\mathbb B}_d\), Stokes' theorem on \(\Omega(s)\setminus r\overline{\mathbb B}_d\) and (A8) give \[ \int_{\partial\Omega(s)}\gamma\wedge(d\gamma)^{d-1}\geq\int_{\partial(r\mathbb B_d)}d^{c}h\wedge(dd^{c}h)^{d-1}=\frac{((1-\eta)k)^{d}}{r^{2d}}\int_{r\mathbb B_d}(dd^{c}|z|^{2})^{d}=((1-\eta)\pi k)^{d}. \] By (16), letting \(\eta\to 0\), we get \[ \int_{\Omega(s)}(dd^{c}|f|^{2})^{d}\geq s^{2d}(\pi k)^{d}. \] Letting \(s\uparrow 1\) along regular values and using monotone convergence gives the claim.2 \(\square\)

The equality case

For a symmetric convex body \(A\), write \(\|x\|_A:=\min\{t\ge0\colon x\in tA\}\) for the norm with unit ball \(A\). For symmetric convex bodies \(A\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\) and \(A'\subset\mathbb{R}^{l}\), define the symmetric convex bodies \[ \begin{aligned} A\oplus_\infty A'&:=A\times A'=\{(x,x')\in\mathbb{R}^{k+l}\colon\max\{\|x\|_A,\|x'\|_{A'}\}\le1\},\\ A\oplus_1A'&:=\operatorname{conv}\big((A\times\{0\})\cup(\{0\}\times A')\big)=\{(x,x')\in\mathbb{R}^{k+l}\colon\|x\|_A+\|x'\|_{A'}\le1\}. \end{aligned} \] Then \((A\oplus_1A')^{\circ}=A^{\circ}\oplus_\infty A'^{\circ}\) and \((A\oplus_\infty A')^{\circ}=A^{\circ}\oplus_1 A'^{\circ}\). Hanner polytopes form the smallest class of convex bodies that contains the segments \([-a,a]\subset\mathbb{R}\), \(a>0\), and is closed under \(\oplus_1\) and \(\oplus_\infty\).

For a general symmetric convex body \(K\), since \(K=(K^{\circ})^{\circ}\) and \(K\) is symmetric, \[ K=\{ x\in \mathbb{R}^{d}\,\,\colon\,\, \langle h,x \rangle \in [-1,1] \quad \forall\,h\in K^{\circ}\}, \] and we define \[ \Omega :=\{ z\in \mathbb{C}^{d}\,\,\colon\,\, \langle h,z \rangle \in D \quad \forall\,h\in K^{\circ}\}. \] For a polytope this agrees with the definition in Complexification: from \(K\) to \(\Omega\), since \(K^{\circ}=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm b_j\}\) and \(\{h\in\mathbb{R}^d\colon\langle h,z\rangle\in D\}\) is convex and symmetric for each \(z\in\mathbb{C}^d\). The set \(\Omega\) is convex and open, \(\Omega\cap\mathbb{R}^d=\operatorname{int}K\), and \(\mathrm{Re}\,z\in K\) for every \(z\in\overline\Omega\). For \(x\in K\), let \(\overline\Omega_x\) be the fiber of \(\overline\Omega\) over \(x\), as before. For \(y\in\mathbb{R}^d\), since \(K^{\circ}\) is symmetric and \(\lambda\) is even, \[ x+iy\in\overline\Omega\iff\langle h,y\rangle\le\lambda(\langle h,x\rangle)\quad\forall\,h\in K^{\circ}.\tag{17} \]

To motivate the next definition, note that \[ K^{\circ}=\bigcup_{x\in K}\operatorname{conv}\big(\{0\}\cup\{h\in K^{\circ}\colon\langle h,x\rangle=1\}\big).\tag{18} \] Indeed, the right-hand side is contained in \(K^\circ\), since \(K^\circ\) is convex and contains \(0\). Conversely, let \(h\in K^\circ\setminus\{0\}\) and let \(x\in K\) maximize \(\langle h,\cdot\rangle\) on \(K\). Then \(t:=\langle h,x\rangle\in(0,1]\), \(h/t\in K^{\circ}\), \(\langle h/t,x\rangle=1\), and \(h\in\operatorname{conv}\{0,h/t\}\).

For \(x\in K\), let \[ \Sigma_x:=\bigcup_{z\in\overline\Omega,\ \mathrm{Re}\,z=x}\operatorname{conv}\big(\{0\}\cup\{h\in K^{\circ}\colon\mathrm{Im}\,\langle h,z\rangle=\lambda(\mathrm{Re}\,\langle h,z\rangle)\}\big)\subseteq K^{\circ},\tag{19} \] and let \[ \Sigma:=\bigcup_{x\in K}\{x\}\times\Sigma_x\subseteq K\times K^{\circ}. \] For \(z\in\overline\Omega\) and \(h\in K^\circ\), the condition in (19) says that \(\langle h,z\rangle\) lies on the upper arc \(\{t+i\lambda(t)\colon|t|\le1\}\) of \(\partial D\). Thus (19) is obtained from (18) by replacing \(K\) with \(\overline\Omega\), and the right endpoint of \([-1,1]\) with the upper arc of \(\partial D\). Note that \(\Sigma_0=K^{\circ}\) by (17) and (18).

If \(K\) is a polytope, \(x\in K_{J,\varepsilon}\) and \(y=y_{J,\varepsilon}(x)\), then \(\langle\varepsilon_jb_j,y\rangle=\lambda(\langle\varepsilon_jb_j,x\rangle)\) for all \(j\in J\), since \(\lambda\) is even, so \(\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\subseteq\Sigma_x\) by (19). Hence, for a polytope \(K\), the set \(\Sigma\) contains the sets \(K_{J,\varepsilon}\times\Delta_{J,\varepsilon}\), so by Lemma 1, (5), (9) and (11), \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma)\ge\frac{4^{d}}{d!}.\tag{20} \]

By (19), \(\xi\in\Sigma_x\) iff \(\xi=\sum_i\alpha_ih_i\) for finitely many \(h_i\in K^{\circ}\) with \(\langle h_i,y\rangle=\lambda(\langle h_i,x\rangle)\) for one \(y\in\overline\Omega_x\) and all \(i\), where \(\alpha_i\ge0\) and \(\sum_i\alpha_i\le1\); by Carathéodory's theorem, \(d+1\) terms suffice. Since \(\lambda\le\lambda(0)\), (17) gives \[ \overline\Omega_x\subseteq\lambda(0)K,\qquad x\in K.\tag{21} \]

Lemma 3

Let \(K^{(j)}\supseteq K\) be symmetric convex bodies with \(K^{(j)}\subseteq t_j^{-1}K\) and \(t_j\to1\), and let \(\Omega^{(j)}\) and \(\Sigma^{(j)}_x\) be defined as \(\Omega\) and \(\Sigma_x\) in (19), with \(K\) replaced by \(K^{(j)}\). If \(x_j\to x\in\operatorname{int} K\), \(\xi_j\to\xi\) and \(\xi_j\in\Sigma^{(j)}_{x_j}\), then \(\xi\in\Sigma_x\).

Proof.

By the description of \(\Sigma^{(j)}_{x_j}\) following (19), we may write \[ \xi_j=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha_{ij}h_{ij},\qquad \alpha_{ij}\ge0,\qquad \sum_{i=0}^d\alpha_{ij}\le1, \] where \(h_{ij}\in (K^{(j)})^{\circ}\subseteq K^{\circ}\) and, for some \(y_j\) with \(x_j+iy_j\in\overline{\Omega^{(j)}}\), \[ \langle h_{ij},y_j\rangle=\lambda(\langle h_{ij},x_j\rangle),\qquad 0\le i\le d. \] By (21) for \(K^{(j)}\), \[ y_j\in\lambda(0)K^{(j)}\subseteq\lambda(0)t_j^{-1}K, \] so all of these data range in a compact set. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that \(y_j\to y\), \(h_{ij}\to h_i\in K^{\circ}\) and \(\alpha_{ij}\to\alpha_i\) for every \(i\). Then \(\alpha_i\ge0\), \(\sum_i\alpha_i\le1\) and \(\xi=\sum_i\alpha_ih_i\).

We claim that \(x+iy\in\overline\Omega\). Let \(h\in K^{\circ}\). Since \(K^{(j)}\subseteq t_j^{-1}K\), we have \(t_jh\in (K^{(j)})^{\circ}\), so by (17) for \(K^{(j)}\), \[ \langle t_jh,y_j\rangle\le\lambda(\langle t_jh,x_j\rangle). \] Letting \(j\to\infty\) and using the continuity of \(\lambda\), we get \(\langle h,y\rangle\le\lambda(\langle h,x\rangle)\). As \(h\in K^\circ\) was arbitrary, (17) gives \(x+iy\in\overline\Omega\). In the same way, letting \(j\to\infty\) in \(\langle h_{ij},y_j\rangle=\lambda(\langle h_{ij},x_j\rangle)\) gives \[ \langle h_i,y\rangle=\lambda(\langle h_i,x\rangle),\qquad 0\le i\le d. \] Therefore \(\xi=\sum_i\alpha_ih_i\in\Sigma_x\) by (19). \(\square\)

Lemma 4

For every symmetric convex body \(K\), the set \(\Sigma\) is measurable and \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma)\ge\frac{4^{d}}{d!}. \] If \(\operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})=4^{d}/d!\), then \(\Sigma_x=K^{\circ}\) for every \(x\in\operatorname{int} K\). In particular, for every \(x\in\operatorname{int} K\) and every \(\xi\in\partial K^{\circ}\) there exist \(y\in\overline\Omega_x\) and vectors \(h_i\in K^{\circ}\) such that \(\xi\in\operatorname{conv}\{h_i\}\) and \(\langle h_i,y\rangle=\lambda(\langle h_i,x\rangle)\) for all \(i\).

Proof.

Take \(K^{(j)}=K\) and \(t_j=1\) in Lemma 3: if \((x_j,\xi_j)\in \Sigma\) and \((x_j,\xi_j)\to(x,\xi)\) with \(x\in\operatorname{int} K\), then \((x,\xi)\in \Sigma\). So \(\Sigma\cap(\operatorname{int} K\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\) is relatively closed in \(\operatorname{int} K\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\), and each fiber \(\Sigma_x\), \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\), is closed; since \(\Sigma_x\subseteq K^{\circ}\), it is compact. The rest of \(\Sigma\) lies in the null set \(\partial K\times K^{\circ}\), so \(\Sigma\) is measurable, and by Fubini \(\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma)=\int_K\operatorname{Vol}(\Sigma_x)\,dx\).

Now choose \(t_j\in(0,1)\) with \(t_j\to1\), and symmetric polytopes \(K^{(j)}\) with \[ K\subseteq K^{(j)}\subseteq t_j^{-1}K\qquad\text{for all }j. \] Write \(\Sigma^{(j)}\) for the set \(\Sigma\) with \(K\) replaced by \(K^{(j)}\). Since \(K^{(j)}\) is a polytope, (20) applies to it, so \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}\big(\Sigma^{(j)}\big)\ge\frac{4^{d}}{d!}\qquad\text{for all }j. \] Put \[ E_j:=\Sigma^{(j)}\cap\big(\operatorname{int} K\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)=\big\{(x,\xi)\,\colon\,x\in\operatorname{int}K,\ \xi\in\Sigma^{(j)}_x\big\}. \] Since \(K\subseteq K^{(j)}\), we have \(\Sigma^{(j)}_x\subseteq(K^{(j)})^{\circ}\subseteq K^{\circ}\) for every \(x\). Hence \(E_j\subseteq K\times K^{\circ}\), and \(\Sigma^{(j)}\setminus E_j\subseteq(K^{(j)}\setminus\operatorname{int}K)\times K^{\circ}\), so \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(E_j)\ge\frac{4^{d}}{d!}-\operatorname{Vol}\big(K^{(j)}\setminus K\big)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})\ge\frac{4^{d}}{d!}-\big(t_j^{-d}-1\big)\operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ}). \] The last term tends to \(0\) as \(j\to\infty\), so by the reverse Fatou lemma \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}\Big(\limsup_{j\to\infty}E_j\Big)\ge\limsup_{j\to\infty}\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(E_j)\ge\frac{4^{d}}{d!}. \] Finally, \(\limsup_jE_j\subseteq \Sigma\). Indeed, let \((x,\xi)\in E_j\) for infinitely many \(j\), say for \(j\) in an infinite set \(J_0\). Then \(x\in\operatorname{int}K\) and \(\xi\in\Sigma^{(j)}_x\) for all \(j\in J_0\), and Lemma 3, applied along \(J_0\) with the constant sequences \(x_j=x\) and \(\xi_j=\xi\), gives \(\xi\in\Sigma_x\), i.e., \((x,\xi)\in \Sigma\). Therefore \[ \operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma)\ge\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}\Big(\limsup_{j\to\infty}E_j\Big)\ge\frac{4^{d}}{d!}, \] which proves the inequality.

Now assume \(\operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})=4^{d}/d!\). Then \(\Sigma\subseteq K\times K^{\circ}\) and \(\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(\Sigma)\ge\operatorname{Vol}_{2d}(K\times K^{\circ})\), so \((\operatorname{int} K\times\operatorname{int} K^{\circ})\setminus \Sigma\) is null. It is open by Lemma 3, hence empty. So \(\Sigma_x\supseteq\operatorname{int} K^{\circ}\), and \(\Sigma_x\) is closed. For \(\xi\in\partial K^{\circ}\), write \(\xi=\sum\alpha_ih_i\) as in the definition (19) of \(\Sigma_x\); the weights \(\alpha_i\) sum to \(1\) since \[1=\|\xi\|_{K^{\circ}}\le\sum\alpha_i\|h_i\|_{K^{\circ}}\le\sum\alpha_i\le1,\] so \(\xi\in\operatorname{conv}\{h_i\}\). \(\square\)

Lemma 5

Proof.

Write \(\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{K^{\circ}}\). It suffices to consider triples of the form \(0,v,u\). We may also assume that the three points are distinct (otherwise any repeated point is a median). The set \[ W:=\{w \in \mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \|v-w\|+\|u-w\|=\|v-u\|\} \] is compact and convex; indeed by the triangle inequality, it is the set of minimizers of the convex function \(\|v-\cdot\|+\|u-\cdot\|\), which tends to \(\infty\) at \(\infty\).

We claim that

\[ \min_{w\in W}\langle w,x\rangle\le\tfrac12\big(\|v\|+\|u\|-\|v-u\|\big)\qquad\text{for every }x\in K.\tag{22} \]

First let us deduce the lemma from (22). Since \(\|w\|=\max_{x\in K}\langle w,x\rangle\), the minimax theorem for the bilinear pairing on the compact convex sets \(W\) and \(K\) gives \[ \min_{w\in W}\|w\|=\max_{x\in K}\min_{w\in W}\langle w,x\rangle\le\tfrac12\big(\|v\|+\|u\|-\|v-u\|\big). \] Pick a minimizer \(w_{0}\); then by the triangle inequality \[ \begin{align*} & 2\big(\|w_{0}\|+\|v-w_{0}\|+\|u-w_{0}\|\big)\\ &\qquad\qquad \le\|v\|+\|u\|+\|v-u\|\\ &\qquad\qquad \le\big(\|w_{0}\|+\|v-w_{0}\|\big)+\big(\|w_{0}\|+\|u-w_{0}\|\big)+\big(\|v-w_{0}\|+\|u-w_{0}\|\big), \end{align*} \] so the three triangle inequalities in the second step are equalities and \(w_{0}\) is a median.

It remains to prove (22). By continuity, we may assume that \(x\in\operatorname{int} K\), so that \(\|x\|_K\lt 1\). Let \[ \widehat K:=K\oplus_1[-1,1]=\{(z,s):\ \|z\|_K+|s|\le1\},\qquad \widehat K^{\circ}=K^{\circ}\times[-1,1]. \] The section of \(\widehat K\) at height \(s\) is \((1-|s|)K\), so \(\operatorname{Vol}(\widehat K)=\frac2{d+1}\operatorname{Vol}(K)\), while \(\operatorname{Vol}(\widehat K^{\circ})=2\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})\). Hence \(\widehat K\) is also an equality case, in dimension \(d+1\). Write \(\widehat\Omega\) for the set \(\Omega\) with the body \(K\) replaced by \(\widehat K\). Fix \(\delta\in(0,1)\) and set \[\hat x:=(\delta x,1-\delta)\in\operatorname{int}\widehat K. \] Let \(\hat v:=(v,\|v\|)\) and \(\hat u:=(u,\|u\|)\) be the lifts of \(v\) and \(u\) to the boundary of the cone \(\{(z,t)\colon\|z\|\le t\}\). Since \(\|(h,s)\|_{\widehat K^{\circ}}=\max\{\|h\|,|s|\}\), we have

Theorem 6 (Lima,3 Hansen–Lima4)

Let \(\|\cdot\|\) be a norm on \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\). Suppose that every triple of points has a median in \((\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|)\). Then the unit ball of \(\|\cdot\|\) is a linear image of a Hanner polytope.

Proof.

A normed space has the 3.2 intersection property if any three pairwise intersecting closed balls, of arbitrary radii, have a common point.5 Let the closed balls of radii \(\rho_i\) centered at \(x_i\), \(i=1,2,3\), intersect pairwise. Let \(m\) be a median of the centers and \(r_i=\|m-x_i\|\), so \(r_i+r_j=\|x_i-x_j\|\le\rho_i+\rho_j\). If every \(r_i\le\rho_i\), then \(m\) is a common point. Otherwise say \(t:=r_1-\rho_1>0\), so \(r_j+t\le\rho_j\) for \(j=2,3\). The point \(z=m+\frac t{r_1}(x_1-m)\) has \(\|z-x_1\|=\rho_1\) and \(\|z-x_j\|\le t+r_j\le\rho_j\). Hence the space has the 3.2 intersection property, and the conclusion is Corollary 7.4 of Hansen–Lima.6 \(\square\)

By Lemma 5 and Theorem 6, if \(\operatorname{Vol}(K)\operatorname{Vol}(K^{\circ})=4^{d}/d!\), then \(K^{\circ}\), and hence \(K\), is a linear image of a Hanner polytope; conversely, induction on the dimension shows that every Hanner polytope has volume product \(4^{d}/d!\). \(\square\)

The integrand \(\operatorname{arctanh}\big(\sin\frac{\pi s}{2}\big)\) is positive on \((0,1)\) and \(O\big(\log\frac{1}{1-s}\big)\) as \(s\uparrow1\), so \(\lambda\) is continuous on \([-1,1]\), \(\lambda(\pm1)=0\), and \(\lambda>0\) on \((-1,1)\). For \(0\lt |t| \lt 1\), since \(\lambda\) is even, \[ \lambda'(t)=-\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{arctanh}\Big(\sin\frac{\pi t}{2}\Big), \] and this also holds at \(t=0\), because the right-hand side is continuous and vanishes there. Differentiating again, \[ \lambda''(t)=-\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}\cos\frac{\pi t}{2}}{1-\sin^{2}\frac{\pi t}{2}}=-\sec\frac{\pi t}{2}\lt0,\qquad -1\lt t \lt 1, \] so \(\lambda\) is strictly concave on \([-1,1]\); being also even, it attains its maximum at \(0\). It follows that \(D\) is a bounded convex domain.

The series converges absolutely on \(\overline{\mathbb D}\), so \(F\) is continuous there, \(F(0)=0\), \(F'(0)=8/\pi^{2}\gt0\), \(F(\pm1)=\pm\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_j(2j+1)^{-2}=\pm1\), and \(F(\bar z)=\overline{F(z)}\). Differentiating the series and multiplying by \(z\), we get \[ zF'(z)=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\operatorname{arctanh}z, \] which extends analytically across \(\partial\mathbb D\setminus\{\pm1\}\). For \(0 \lt \theta\lt\pi\), \(\operatorname{arctanh}e^{i\theta}=\frac12\log\big(i\cot\frac\theta2\big)\), so \[ \frac{d}{d\theta}F(e^{i\theta})=\frac{8i}{\pi^{2}}\operatorname{arctanh}e^{i\theta}=-\frac{2}{\pi}+\frac{4i}{\pi^{2}}\log\cot\frac{\theta}{2}. \] Hence \(\mathrm{Re}\,F(e^{i\theta})=1-2\theta/\pi=:t\), and, by \(\log\cot\frac{x}{2}=\operatorname{arctanh}(\cos x)\), \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Im}\,F=-\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{arctanh}\big(\sin\frac{\pi t}{2}\big)=\lambda'(t)\) with \(\mathrm{Im}\,F=0\) at \(t=\pm1\).

So \(\mathrm{Im}\,F(e^{i\theta})=\lambda(t)\), and since \(F(\bar z)=\overline{F(z)}\), \[ F(e^{\pm i\theta})=t\pm i\lambda(t),\qquad t=1-\frac{2\theta}{\pi},\quad 0\le\theta\le\pi. \] Thus \(F\) maps the upper and the lower semicircle bijectively onto the upper and the lower half of \(\partial D\), and \(F|_{\partial\mathbb D}\) is a homeomorphism onto the Jordan curve \(\partial D\), which it traverses counterclockwise.

Let \(w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\partial D\). As \(r\uparrow1\), the curves \(\theta\mapsto F(re^{i\theta})\) converge uniformly to \(\theta\mapsto F(e^{i\theta})\), so for \(r\) close to \(1\) their winding number around \(w\) is \(1\) if \(w\in D\) and \(0\) if \(w\notin\overline D\). By the argument principle, \(F\) takes every value in \(D\) exactly once in \(\mathbb D\) and no value outside \(\overline D\). Since \(F(\mathbb D)\) is open, it does not meet \(\partial D\). Hence \(F\) is a biholomorphism of \(\mathbb D\) onto \(D\), and, with the boundary correspondence, a continuous bijection of the compact set \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) onto \(\overline D\), hence a homeomorphism.

It remains to prove the endpoint asymptotics. Substituting \(s=1-\tau\) in the definition of \(\lambda\) and using \(\operatorname{arctanh}(\cos u)=\log\cot\frac u2\), \[ \lambda(1-\delta)=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\delta}\log\cot\frac{\pi\tau}{4}\,d\tau. \] Since \(\log\cot u=\log\frac1u+O(u^2)\) for \(0\lt u\le\frac\pi4\), the integral equals \(\int_0^\delta\log\frac{4}{\pi\tau}\,d\tau+O(\delta^3)=\delta\log\frac1\delta+O(\delta)\). \(\square\)

Appendix: Complex-geometric background

This appendix collects the notation and the standard facts used in From the torus to the polydisc to \(\Omega\). Throughout, \(z_j=x_j+iy_j\) are the coordinates of \(\mathbb{C}^d\), and \(\mathbb{C}^d\) is oriented by \(dV=\prod_j dx_j\wedge dy_j\).

Differential forms. A smooth function \(u\) on an open set of \(\mathbb{C}^d\) has \(du=\partial u+\bar\partial u\), where \[ \partial u=\sum_j\frac{\partial u}{\partial z_j}\,dz_j,\qquad \bar\partial u=\sum_j\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bar z_j}\,d\bar z_j,\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial z_j}=\frac12\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial y_j}\Big),\quad \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar z_j}=\frac12\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial y_j}\Big). \] The operators \(\partial,\bar\partial\) extend to forms, and \(\partial^2=\bar\partial^2=0\), \(\partial\bar\partial=-\bar\partial\partial\). A function is holomorphic if and only if \(\bar\partial u=0\). For the operator \(d^c=\frac i4(\bar\partial-\partial)\), a one-line computation gives \[ dd^c=\frac i4(\partial+\bar\partial)(\bar\partial-\partial)=\frac i2\,\partial\bar\partial,\qquad dd^cu=\frac i2\sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k}\,dz_j\wedge d\bar z_k.\tag{A1} \] The matrix \(\big(\partial^2u/\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k\big)\) is the complex Hessian (Levi matrix) of \(u\). Since \(\frac i2\,dz_j\wedge d\bar z_j=dx_j\wedge dy_j\), (A1) gives \(dd^c|z|^2=\sum_jdx_j\wedge dy_j\), and since \(d^c\) and \(d\) are real operators, \(dd^cu\) is a real \((1,1)\)-form. Taking the \(d\)-th exterior power in (A1) gives, for smooth \(u\), \[ (dd^cu)^d=d!\,\det\Big(\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k}\Big)\,dV.\tag{A2} \] For \(u=|z|^2\) the matrix is the identity, which gives \((dd^c|z|^2)^d=d!\,dV\). In particular, if \(r\mathbb{B}_d\) is the ball of radius \(r\) in \(\mathbb{C}^d\), whose volume is \(\pi^dr^{2d}/d!\), then \[ \int_{r\mathbb{B}_d}(dd^c|z|^2)^d=(\pi r^2)^d.\tag{A3} \]

Pullbacks. If \(\Psi\) is a holomorphic map between open subsets of complex Euclidean spaces, then pullback by \(\Psi\) commutes with \(\partial\) and \(\bar\partial\), hence with \(d\), \(d^c\) and \(dd^c\). In particular \(dd^c|f|^2=f^*(dd^c|w|^2)\) for holomorphic \(f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}^m\). Moreover, a holomorphic map \(\Psi\colon\mathbb{C}^d\supseteq U\to\mathbb{C}^d\) has real Jacobian determinant \(|\det D\Psi|^2\), where \(D\Psi\) is the complex Jacobian matrix, so \[ \Psi^*dV=|\det D\Psi|^2\,dV.\tag{A4} \] Finally, recall that \(dV_J=\bigwedge_{j\in J}\frac i2dw_j\wedge d\bar w_j\) is the volume form of \(\mathbb{C}^J\), where \(J\subseteq[m]\), \(|J|=d\). Expanding \((dd^c|w|^2)^d=\big(\sum_{j=1}^m\frac i2dw_j\wedge d\bar w_j\big)^d\), each of the \(2\)-forms \(\frac i2dw_j\wedge d\bar w_j\) commutes with the others and squares to zero, so only products of \(d\) distinct ones survive, and each such product occurs \(d!\) times: \[ (dd^c|w|^2)^d=d!\sum_{J\in{[m]\choose d}}dV_J\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{C}^m.\tag{A5} \] Combining (A4) and (A5) with \(\pi_J\circ f\) in place of \(\Psi\) gives \((dd^c|f|^2)^d=d!\sum_J|\det(Df)_J|^2\,dV\) for holomorphic \(f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}^m\), the Cauchy–Binet formula.

Plurisubharmonic functions. A smooth real function \(u\) on an open set of \(\mathbb{C}^d\) is plurisubharmonic (psh) if its complex Hessian is positive semidefinite at every point; equivalently, \(\sum u_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k\ge0\) for all \(\xi\in\mathbb{C}^d\), that is, the restriction of \(u\) to every complex line is subharmonic. Facts used:

  1. (Positivity.) If \(u\) is smooth and psh, then \(\det(u_{j\bar k})\ge0\), so \((dd^cu)^d\ge0\) by (A2).
  2. (Holomorphic images.) If \(f\) is holomorphic and nonvanishing on an open set, then \(\log|f|^2\) is smooth and psh there: for a vector \(\xi\), writing \(a=Df\,\xi\), its Levi form is \[ \frac{|f|^2|a|^2-|\langle a,f\rangle|^2}{|f|^4}\ \ge0 \] by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Also \(|f|^2\) is psh (its Levi form is \(|a|^2\)), and \(c\log|z|^2+\text{const}\) is psh on \(\mathbb{C}^d\setminus\{0\}\) for \(c\ge0\), as the case \(f(z)=z\).
  3. (Convex functions of psh functions.) If \(u_1,u_2\) are smooth and psh and \(\chi\colon\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\) is smooth, convex, and nondecreasing in each variable, then \(\chi(u_1,u_2)\) is smooth and psh. Indeed, by the chain rule its complex Hessian is \(\sum_a\chi_a\,\mathrm{Hess}_{\mathbb{C}}u_a+\sum_{a,b}\chi_{ab}\,\partial u_a\otimes\bar\partial u_b\), where \(\chi_a\ge0\) by monotonicity and \((\chi_{ab})\ge0\) by convexity.
  4. (Regularized maximum.) Besides being smooth, convex and nondecreasing in each variable, \(\widetilde{\max}\) is only required to satisfy \(\widetilde{\max}(x,y)=\max(x,y)\) when \(|x-y|\ge1\); so \(\widetilde{\max}(g,h)=g\) where \(g\ge h+1\), and \(=h\) where \(h\ge g+1\). Such a function is obtained by convolving \(\max\) with a smooth, nonnegative, radially symmetric function of integral \(1\) supported in the disc of radius \(1/2\): convexity and monotonicity are preserved, and if \(|x-y|\ge1\), then \(\max\) is affine on the disc of radius \(1/2\) about \((x,y)\), so its value at \((x,y)\) is unchanged. See also the footnote at the end of the proof of Lemma 2.

Stokes' theorem and regular values. Let \(f\colon \Omega\to\mathbb{C}^m\) be smooth, and let \(s>0\). If \(s\) is a regular value of \(|f|\), meaning that \(d|f|\neq0\) at every point of \(\{|f|=s\}\), then \(\{|f|\le s\}\) is a manifold with boundary \(\partial\Omega(s)=\{|f|=s\}\), which is compact when \(\Omega(s)\) is relatively compact in \(\Omega\) and \(\{|f|\le s\}\) is closed in \(\Omega\). By Sard's theorem, almost every \(s\) is a regular value. Boundaries carry the orientation induced from \(\mathbb{C}^d\) (outward normal first), so for a smooth \((2d-1)\)-form \(\beta\) on a neighborhood of \(\overline{\Omega(s)}\), \[ \int_{\Omega(s)}d\beta=\int_{\partial\Omega(s)}\beta.\tag{A6} \] The same holds on the region \(\Omega(s)\setminus r\overline{\mathbb{B}}_d\) between two such boundaries, whose oriented boundary is \(\partial\Omega(s)\) minus \(\partial(r\mathbb{B}_d)\), where \(\partial(r\mathbb{B}_d)\) is oriented as the boundary of the ball. This is how (A6) is used to compare the two boundary integrals in the proof. Two further remarks about forms restricted to a hypersurface \(\{\varrho=\text{const}\}\): the restriction of \(d\varrho\) to the hypersurface vanishes, so \[ \big(d\log\varrho\wedge d^c\log\varrho\big)\big|_{\{\varrho=\text{const}\}}=0,\tag{A7} \] which is why \(dd^c\log|f|^2\) and \(|f|^{-2}dd^c|f|^2\) agree on \(\partial\Omega(s)\) (take \(\varrho=|f|^2\)) and likewise \(dd^c\log|z|^2=r^{-2}dd^c|z|^2\) on the sphere \(\partial(r\mathbb{B}_d)\); and, by (A6) applied to the ball and (A3), \[ \int_{\partial(r\mathbb{B}_d)}d^c|z|^2\wedge(dd^c|z|^2)^{d-1}=\int_{r\mathbb{B}_d}(dd^c|z|^2)^d=(\pi r^2)^d.\tag{A8} \] Here we used \(d\big(d^c|z|^2\wedge(dd^c|z|^2)^{d-1}\big)=(dd^c|z|^2)^d\), valid because \(dd^c\) of anything is closed.

Other notation. \(\mathbb{B}_d\) denotes the open Euclidean unit ball of \(\mathbb{C}^d\); \(|\cdot|\) is the Hermitian norm; \(\overline{\Omega(s)}\) is the closure; \(g=O(1)\) means that \(g\) is bounded near the point in question; and \(Df\) is the complex Jacobian of a holomorphic map \(f\), with \((Df)_J\) its \(d\times d\) minor with rows in \(J\).
